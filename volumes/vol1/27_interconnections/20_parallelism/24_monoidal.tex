\subsection{Formal definition}

\begin{ctdefinition}[Strict monoidal category]
    \label{def:strict-monoidal-cat}
    A \maindef{strict monoidal category} is a category $\CatC$ equipped with a \emph{strict monoidal structure}. A \emph{strict monoidal structure} is

    \constit

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item a functor $\mtimescat \colon \CatC \Ctimes \CatC \fto \CatC$, called the \emph{monoidal product}
        \item an object $\idmoncat \setin \Obof{\CatC}$, called the \emph{monoidal unit};
    \end{enumerate}

    \condit

    \begin{enumerate}
    \item the monoidal product is associative on objects: 
    \begin{equation}
     (\Obja \mtimescat \Objb) \mtimescat \Objc = \Obja \mtimescat (\Objb \mtimescat \Objc);
    \end{equation}

    \item the monoidal product is associative on morphisms,
    \begin{equation}
     (\mora \mtimescat \morb) \mtimescat \morc = \mora \mtimescat (\morb \mtimescat \morc);
    \end{equation}
    
        \item  the monoidal unit is neutral on the level of objects,
              \begin{equation}\label{eq:strict-mon-stacking-cat-cond1}
                  \Obja \mtimescatob \idmoncat = \Obja \qquad \text{and} \qquad \idmoncat \mtimescatob \Obja = \Obja;
              \end{equation}
        \item the monoidal unit is neutral on the level of morphisms,
              \begin{equation}\label{eq:strict-mon-stacking-cat-cond2}
                  \mora \mtimescatmor \catid_\idmoncat = \mora \qquad \text{and} \qquad \catid_\idmoncat \mtimescatmor  \mora = \mora.
              \end{equation}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{ctdefinition}

\subsection{Basic examples}

\begin{example}[Real matrices between coordinate spaces]
    \label{exa:MatR-strict-monoidal}
    Recall the category $\MatR$ of real matrices (\cref{def:MatR}): its objects are the real coordinate spaces $\reals^n$, for $n \setin \natnumbers$, a morphism $\matmor{A} \colon \reals^k \mto \reals^m$ is an $m \times k$ real matrix, and composition is given by the matrix product, $\matmor{A} \mthen \matmor{B} = \matmor{B}\matmor{A}$.

    The following defines a strict monoidal structure on $\MatR$.

    On objects, the monoidal product sums the dimensions:
    \begin{equation}
        \reals^k \mtimescat \reals^l \definedas \reals^{k+l}.
    \end{equation}
    On morphisms, given $\matmor{A} \colon \reals^k \mto \reals^m$ and $\matmor{B} \colon \reals^l \mto \reals^n$, we define $\matmor{A} \mtimescat \matmor{B} \colon \reals^{k+l} \mto \reals^{m+n}$ to be the block-diagonal matrix
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:MatR-monoidal-product}
        \matmor{A} \mtimescat \matmor{B} \definedas
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \matmor{A} & \mat{0}    \\
            \mat{0}    & \matmor{B}
        \end{bmatrix},
    \end{equation}
    which has $m + n$ rows and $k + l$ columns.
    In terms of the linear functions which the matrices encode, this is parallel composition in a very literal sense: if we stack a vector $\vectob{v} \setin \reals^k$ on top of a vector $\vectob{w} \setin \reals^l$, then
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \matmor{A} & \mat{0}    \\
            \mat{0}    & \matmor{B}
        \end{bmatrix}
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \vectob{v} \\
            \vectob{w}
        \end{bmatrix}
        =
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \matmor{A}\vectob{v} \\
            \matmor{B}\vectob{w}
        \end{bmatrix},
    \end{equation}
    so $\matmor{A}$ acts on the upper part, $\matmor{B}$ acts on the lower part, and the two do not interact.

    The monoidal unit is $\idmoncat \definedas \reals^0$.
    Its identity morphism is the matrix with zero rows and zero columns.

    Let us check the conditions.
    The monoidal product is a functor: for matrices $\matmor{A} \colon \reals^k \mto \reals^m$, $\matmor{C} \colon \reals^m \mto \reals^p$, $\matmor{B} \colon \reals^l \mto \reals^n$, and $\matmor{D} \colon \reals^n \mto \reals^q$, multiplying block-diagonal matrices block by block gives
    \begin{equation}
        (\matmor{A} \mtimescat \matmor{B}) \mthen (\matmor{C} \mtimescat \matmor{D})
        =
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \matmor{C} & \mat{0}    \\
            \mat{0}    & \matmor{D}
        \end{bmatrix}
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \matmor{A} & \mat{0}    \\
            \mat{0}    & \matmor{B}
        \end{bmatrix}
        =
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \matmor{C}\matmor{A} & \mat{0}              \\
            \mat{0}              & \matmor{D}\matmor{B}
        \end{bmatrix}
        =
        (\matmor{A} \mthen \matmor{C}) \mtimescat (\matmor{B} \mthen \matmor{D}),
    \end{equation}
    and the block-diagonal matrix built from two identity matrices is an identity matrix, $\idmat_k \mtimescat \idmat_l = \idmat_{k+l}$.
    The monoidal product is associative on objects because addition of natural numbers is, $\reals^{(k+l)+p} = \reals^{k+(l+p)}$, and it is associative on morphisms because both ways of bracketing give the same block-diagonal matrix:
    \begin{equation}
        (\matmor{A} \mtimescat \matmor{B}) \mtimescat \matmor{C}
        =
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \matmor{A} & \mat{0}    & \mat{0}    \\
            \mat{0}    & \matmor{B} & \mat{0}    \\
            \mat{0}    & \mat{0}    & \matmor{C}
        \end{bmatrix}
        =
        \matmor{A} \mtimescat (\matmor{B} \mtimescat \matmor{C}).
    \end{equation}
    Finally, the monoidal unit is neutral on objects, since $\reals^{k+0} = \reals^k = \reals^{0+k}$, and on morphisms, since adding a block with zero rows and zero columns to a matrix does not change it.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
    \label{rem:LinRel-strict-monoidal}
    There is a category of real \emph{linear relations} which is a strict monoidal category in a very similar way.
    Its objects are again the coordinate spaces $\reals^n$, but a morphism from $\reals^k$ to $\reals^m$ is now a linear subspace of $\reals^{k+m}$, thought of as a relation between vectors in $\reals^k$ and vectors in $\reals^m$, and morphisms are composed like relations.
    The monoidal product again sums the dimensions on objects, and it places two linear relations side by side, just as \cref{eq:MatR-monoidal-product} does for matrices.
    We do not spell out the details here; see also \cref{rem:dimension-trick}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Examples where the objects are natural numbers}
\label{sec:strict-monoidal-natural-numbers}

In many strict monoidal categories, the objects are simply the natural numbers, which count how many wires of a single kind there are.
In all the examples of this subsection, the monoidal product on objects is addition of natural numbers, and the monoidal unit is $0 \setin \natnumbers$.
Addition is associative and has $0$ as neutral element, so the conditions on the level of objects in \cref{def:strict-monoidal-cat} hold for free, and an example is specified by saying what the morphisms are, how they are composed, and what their monoidal product is.

Some of the strict monoidal categories of this kind are ``stand-ins'' for larger and more complicated categories.
The idea is that the number $n$ stands for a typical object of ``size'' $n$ in the larger category, for instance for a set with $n$ elements, or for a space of dimension $n$.
If all objects of the same size are interchangeable for our purposes, then we lose nothing by keeping just one number for each size, and we gain a lot: a monoidal product which, in the larger category, is associative only up to renaming or re-bracketing becomes addition of numbers, which is associative on the nose.
We will point out what stands for what in the examples below.

\begin{example}[Functions between finite sets]
    \label{exa:prop-finset}
    There is a strict monoidal category $\stylecat{FinSet}$ of this kind, where
    \begin{itemize}
        \item the set of morphisms from $m$ to $n$ (for $m, n \setin \natnumbers$) is defined to be the set of functions from $\makeset{1, \dots, m}$ to $\makeset{1, \dots, n}$;
        \item composition is the usual composition of functions;
        \item identity morphisms are identity functions;
        \item the monoidal product of functions $\mora \colon \makeset{1, \dots, m} \mto \makeset{1, \dots, n}$ and $\mora\stylemorph{'} \colon \makeset{1, \dots, m'} \mto \makeset{1, \dots, n'}$ is the ``disjoint union''
              \begin{equation}
                  \mora + \mora\stylemorph{'} \colon \makeset{1, \dots, m + m'} \mto \makeset{1, \dots, n + n'},
              \end{equation}
              which applies $\mora$ to the first $m$ numbers, and applies $\mora\stylemorph{'}$, shifted by $m$ in the source and by $n$ in the target, to the remaining $m'$ numbers.
    \end{itemize}

    This category is a stand-in for the category of all finite sets and all functions between them, with the sum of sets and of functions as parallel composition (\cref{sec:stacking}).
    The number $n$ stands for the set $\makeset{1, \dots, n}$, and thereby for every set with $n$ elements: any such set can be put in bijection with $\makeset{1, \dots, n}$ by numbering its elements, and after numbering the elements, a function between two finite sets becomes a morphism of $\stylecat{FinSet}$.
    The sum of a set with $m$ elements and a set with $n$ elements has $m + n$ elements, which is why addition appears as the monoidal product on objects.
    But while the sum of sets is associative only up to renaming the elements, addition of numbers is associative on the nose.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Polynomial maps]
    \label{exa:polynomial-maps}
    There is a strict monoidal category $\stylecat{Poly}_\reals$ of this kind, where
    \begin{itemize}
        \item a morphism from $m$ to $n$ is a tuple $\stylemorph{p} = \tup{p_1, \dots, p_n}$ of $n$ polynomials with real coefficients in the $m$ variables $x_1, \dots, x_m$;
        \item composition is \emph{substitution}: given $\stylemorph{p} = \tup{p_1, \dots, p_n} \colon \styleobj{m} \mto \styleobj{n}$ and $\stylemorph{q} = \tup{q_1, \dots, q_k} \colon \styleobj{n} \mto \styleobj{k}$, the composite $\stylemorph{p} \mthen \stylemorph{q} \colon \styleobj{m} \mto \styleobj{k}$ is the tuple of polynomials
              \begin{equation}
                  \label{eq:polynomial-maps-composition}
                  q_j(p_1, \dots, p_n), \qquad j = 1, \dots, k,
              \end{equation}
              which are obtained by substituting the polynomial $p_i$ for the $i$-th variable of $q_j$, for all $i$;
        \item the identity morphism of $n$ is the tuple of variables $\tup{x_1, \dots, x_n}$;
        \item the monoidal product of $\stylemorph{p} = \tup{p_1, \dots, p_n} \colon \styleobj{m} \mto \styleobj{n}$ and $\stylemorph{p}\stylemorph{'} = \tup{p'_1, \dots, p'_{n'}} \colon \styleobj{m'} \mto \styleobj{n'}$ is the tuple
              \begin{equation}
                  \stylemorph{p} \mtimescatmor \stylemorph{p}\stylemorph{'} \definedas \tup{p_1, \dots, p_n, \tilde{p}'_1, \dots, \tilde{p}'_{n'}} \colon \styleobj{m + m'} \mto \styleobj{n + n'},
              \end{equation}
              where $\tilde{p}'_j$ is the polynomial $p'_j$ with its variables $x_1, \dots, x_{m'}$ renamed to $x_{m+1}, \dots, x_{m+m'}$, so that the two tuples use different variables.
    \end{itemize}
    For instance, composing $\tup{x_1 + x_2,\ x_1 x_2} \colon \styleobj{2} \mto \styleobj{2}$ with $\tup{x_1^2 - x_2} \colon \styleobj{2} \mto \styleobj{1}$ gives
    \begin{equation}
        \tup{(x_1 + x_2)^2 - x_1 x_2} = \tup{x_1^2 + x_1 x_2 + x_2^2} \colon \styleobj{2} \mto \styleobj{1}.
    \end{equation}

    This category, too, is a stand-in.
    The number $n$ stands for the coordinate space $\reals^n$, and a morphism $\stylemorph{p} \colon \styleobj{m} \mto \styleobj{n}$ stands for the function $\reals^m \sto \reals^n$ which maps a vector $\tup{x_1, \dots, x_m}$ to the vector with the entries $p_1(x_1, \dots, x_m), \dots, p_n(x_1, \dots, x_m)$.
    Substitution of polynomials corresponds to the composition of these functions, and the monoidal product corresponds to letting two such functions act side by side on two blocks of coordinates.
    So we may think of $\stylecat{Poly}_\reals$ as the category of coordinate spaces and polynomial functions between them, described purely in terms of formulas.

    Note what happens when all the polynomials are linear without constant term, such as $3 x_1 - x_2$.
    A tuple of $n$ such polynomials in $m$ variables is the same thing as an $n \times m$ matrix of coefficients, and substituting such tuples into each other is precisely matrix multiplication.
    In this way, the category of matrices sits inside $\stylecat{Poly}_\reals$; see also the next remark.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Keeping track only of the dimension]
    \label{rem:dimension-trick}
    For various strict monoidal categories whose objects are the coordinate spaces $\reals^n$, we can apply the same ``trick'' and keep track only of the dimension: instead of the object $\reals^n$, we use the natural number $n$.
    Nothing is lost, since the space $\reals^n$ is determined by $n$, and since $\reals^{m} \mtimescatob \reals^{n} = \reals^{m+n}$ corresponds to the addition $m + n$.

    As an illustration, consider the strict monoidal category $\MatR$ of real matrices (\cref{exa:MatR-strict-monoidal}).
    Applying the trick gives a strict monoidal category $\stylecat{Mat}_\reals$ whose objects are the natural numbers, in which a morphism from $m$ to $n$ is an $n \times m$ real matrix, composition is the matrix product (in the order $\matmor{A} \mthen \matmor{B} = \matmor{B}\matmor{A}$), identity morphisms are identity matrices, and the monoidal product of two matrices is the block-diagonal matrix which they form.
    The two categories differ only in how their objects are named.

    The same can be done for the category of linear relations (\cref{rem:LinRel-strict-monoidal}), which gives a strict monoidal category $\stylecat{LinRel}_\reals$ whose objects are natural numbers, and for LTI systems.
    Which of the two versions one uses is a matter of taste: writing $\reals^n$ is a reminder of what the wires carry, while writing $n$ is shorter.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Signal-flow diagrams]
    \label{exa:signal-flow-diagrams}
    In control theory, linear systems are often described by \emph{signal-flow diagrams}.
    These are built from a small number of basic components, each of which has a certain number of input wires and of output wires, all carrying real-valued signals:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item for every real number $c$, an \emph{amplifier}, which multiplies a signal by $c$ (one input, one output);
        \item an \emph{adder}, which adds two signals (two inputs, one output);
        \item a \emph{branch point}, which copies a signal (one input, two outputs);
        \item a \emph{zero source}, which emits the signal $0$ (no input, one output);
        \item a \emph{terminator}, which discards a signal (one input, no output).
    \end{itemize}
    There is a strict monoidal category $\stylecat{SigFlow}$ of the kind considered in this subsection, where
    \begin{itemize}
        \item a morphism from $m$ to $n$ is a diagram with $m$ input wires and $n$ output wires which is built from the basic components by connecting them in series and by placing them on top of each other;
        \item composition connects the output wires of one diagram to the input wires of the next;
        \item the identity morphism of $n$ consists of $n$ wires, without any components;
        \item the monoidal product places one diagram on top of the other.
    \end{itemize}
    For instance, the following diagram, built from two amplifiers and an adder, is a morphism $\styleobj{2} \mto \styleobj{1}$:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:signal-flow-example}
        \begin{tikzpicture}[baseline=(current bounding box.center)]
            \node[block, minimum width=7.5mm, minimum height=6.5mm, inner sep=2pt] (a) at (0,0.5) {$\stylemorph{a}$};
            \node[block, minimum width=7.5mm, minimum height=6.5mm, inner sep=2pt] (b) at (0,-0.5) {$\stylemorph{b}$};
            \node[block, minimum width=7.5mm, minimum height=16.5mm, inner sep=2pt] (s) at (1.9,0) {$\stylemorph{+}$};
            \draw[draw=objects, thick] (a.west) -- ++(-1.1,0);
            \draw[draw=objects, thick] (b.west) -- ++(-1.1,0);
            \draw[draw=objects, thick] (a.east) -- (s.west |- a);
            \draw[draw=objects, thick] (b.east) -- (s.west |- b);
            \draw[draw=objects, thick] (s.east) -- ++(1.1,0);
        \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{equation}
    It describes the system which turns two input signals $u_1$ and $u_2$ into the output signal $a u_1 + b u_2$.

    This is our first example of a strict monoidal category in which the morphisms are themselves pictures.
    Two diagrams count as the same morphism if they differ only in how they are drawn; we will make this precise in \cref{sec:graphical-calculus}.
    Note also that, for now, the wires of a diagram are not allowed to cross, and there are no feedback loops.
    Both will become available later, once we have symmetric strict monoidal categories and feedback.

    A diagram is only a description of a system; the system itself is a linear function.
    Every diagram from $m$ to $n$ determines an $n \times m$ real matrix, which tells us how the output signals depend on the input signals: for the diagram in \cref{eq:signal-flow-example}, it is the matrix $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \end{bmatrix}$.
    Connecting diagrams in series corresponds to multiplying their matrices, and placing them on top of each other corresponds to forming a block-diagonal matrix.
    Different diagrams can have the same matrix: for example, a branch point followed by an adder has the same matrix as the amplifier for $c = 2$.
    So $\stylecat{SigFlow}$ and the category $\stylecat{Mat}_\reals$ of \cref{rem:dimension-trick} are two different strict monoidal categories, one of descriptions and one of the things described.
\end{example}

\subsection{Examples where the monoidal product is like a cartesian product}
\label{sec:strict-monoidal-lists}

Our first model of parallel composition was the \SY{cartesian product} of sets and functions (\cref{sec:modeling-parallelism}): placing two systems side by side yields a system whose inputs are pairs of inputs, and whose outputs are pairs of outputs.
The \SY{cartesian product}, however, is not associative on the nose: the elements of $(\setA \cartprod \setB) \cartprod \setC$ and of $\setA \cartprod (\setB \cartprod \setC)$ are differently nested pairs (\cref{eq:assoc-fails1}).
Nor is a set with one element neutral for it on the nose.
So $\Set$ with the \SY{cartesian product} is not a strict monoidal category in the sense of \cref{def:strict-monoidal-cat}.

In this subsection we present a simple repair: we replace nested pairs by flat tuples.
Concatenation of tuples is associative on the nose, and the empty tuple is neutral for it, just as for lists (\cref{exa:string-monoid}).
Hence, if the objects of a category are sets of tuples, and the monoidal product concatenates tuples, then the conditions on the level of objects in \cref{def:strict-monoidal-cat} hold for free.
We first carry this out for sets and functions.
This gives a strict monoidal category $\SetL$ which does the same job as $\Set$ with the \SY{cartesian product}.
The same recipe then applies to preorders, to relations, to monotone relations, and to Boolean profunctors.
In all of these examples, an element of a monoidal product of two objects consists of an element of the first object \emph{and} an element of the second one, just as for the \SY{cartesian product}.

\paragraph{The category \SetL}
\label{sec:cartcatset}

We would like a strict monoidal structure on $\Set$ which is modeled on the \SY{cartesian product}.
The idea is the following.
Instead of forming the product of $n$ sets by taking the \SY{cartesian product} of two sets at a time, which leads to nested pairs, we form it in one go: as the set of all tuples $\tup{\elna{1}, \ldots, \elna{n}}$ whose entries are taken from the $n$ given sets $\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{n}$.
Such a set of tuples is an honest set, and so these sets, together with all functions between them, form a \SY{subcategory} of $\Set$.
On this subcategory there is a product of sets which does the same job as the \SY{cartesian product}, but which concatenates tuples instead of nesting them, and which is therefore associative on the nose.
In terms of wires, a set of tuples of length $n$ stands for $n$ parallel wires, one for each entry, and the wires can interact: a function of two arguments, for example, is a morphism from a set of tuples of length two to a set of tuples of length one.

Concretely, we will define a category~\SetL whose objects are ``sets of tuples''.

\begin{ctdefinition}[\SetL]\label{def:SetL}
    \SYNDEF{category of tuple-sets and functions}
    The category \SetL is the category whose objects are those sets which are of the form~$\cObj{\setAn{1}, \setAn{2}, \ldots, \setAn{n}}$, for any~$n \setin \natnumbers$ and any sets~$\setAn{1}, \setAn{2}, \ldots, \setAn{n}$.
    Morphisms in \SetL are any functions between such sets, and the composition operations and identities are the usual ones for functions.
\end{ctdefinition}

\paragraph{From \Set to~\SetL}

Observe that we can turn any set into an object of \SetL: given a set~\setA, there is the associated set
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:setintosetl}
    \cObj{\setA} = \makeset{ \tup{\ela} \mid \ela \setin \setA }
\end{equation}
whose elements are precisely all the tuples of length one whose entry is an element of~\setA.
This defines a function
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:setintosetlfunction}
    \begin{aligned}
        \Obof{\Set} & \to \Obof{\SetL} \\
        \setA       & \mapsto \cObj{\setA}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
%\Ob_{\Set} \to \Ob_{\SetL}, \setA \mapsto \cObj{\setA}
which one might call ``bracket''.

Similarly, given a function~$\mapa \colon \setA \sto \setB$ between sets, there is an associated function~$\cMor{ \mapa }\colon \cObj{\setA} \mto \cObj{\setB}$ given by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:functionintosetlmor}
    \cMor{ \mapa } ( \tup{\ela }) \definedas \tup{\mapa(\ela)}.
\end{equation}
This defines a function
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:functionintosetlmorfun}
    \Hom_{\Set}(\setA,\setB) \to \Hom_{\SetL}(\cObj{\setA},\cObj{\setB})
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Multiplication in \SetL}
\label{sec:multiplication-for-SetL}
We define a multiplication for objects of \SetL.
The symbol~$\cprod$ will denote this multiplication in infix notation.

Given~$\cObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{m}}$ and~$\cObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{n}}$ we define the operation
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:mult-setl-sign}
    \cprod \colon \Obof{\SetL} \cartprod \Obof{\SetL} \to \Obof{\SetL}
\end{equation}
by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:strict-monoidal-product-sets}
    \makecprod{
        \cObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{m}},
        \cObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{n}}
    } \definedas \cObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{m}, \setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{n}}
\end{equation}
for any $n, m \setin \natnumbers$.

The elements of~$\cObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{m}} \cprod \cObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{n}}$ are all possible concatenations of elements of~$\cObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{m}}$ with elements of~$\cObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{n}}$.

Equation \cref{eq:strict-monoidal-product-sets} holds in particular also in the cases when~$m = 0$ or~$n =0$:
\begin{equation}
    \makecprod{
        \emptycobj,
        \cObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{n}}
    } =
    \cObj{
        \setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{n}
    }
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \makecprod{
        \cObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{m}},
        \emptycobj
    } =
    \cObj{
        \setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{m}
    }.
\end{equation}

The multiplication of objects in~\SetL is \SY{associative} (which was our goal with making this construction).
Indeed, both
\begin{equation}
    \makecprod{
        ( \makecprod{\cObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{l}}, \cObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{m}}})
        ,
        \cObj{\setCn{1}, \ldots, \setCn{n}}
    }
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \makecprod{
        \cObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{l}},
        (
        \makecprod{
            \cObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{m}},
            \cObj{\setCn{1}, \ldots, \setCn{n}}
        }
        )
    }
\end{equation}
are equal to
\begin{equation}
    \cObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{l}, \setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{m}, \setCn{1}, \ldots, \setCn{n}}.
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}
    Note that, for any sets $\setA, \setB$, we have
    \begin{equation}
        \cObj{\setA, \setB} = \setA \cartprod \setB.
    \end{equation}
    In other words, the multiplication operation in $\SetL$ ``coincides'' with the cartesian product operation in the special case when we are multiplying two sets together.

    Because of this, the operation of cartesian product is well-defined for $\SetL$.
    Namely, given tuples sets $\cObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{l}}$ and $\cObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{m}}$, their cartesian product is again a tuple set:
    \begin{equation}
        \cObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{l}} \cartprod \cObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{m}} = \cObj{\cObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{l}}, \cObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{m}}},
    \end{equation}
    (which is a ``nested'' tuple set).

\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:SetL-strict-monoidal}
    The following defines a strict monoidal structure on $\SetL$:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item on objects, the monoidal product is the multiplication $\cprod$ of \cref{eq:strict-monoidal-product-sets};
        \item on morphisms, the monoidal product is defined as follows: for $\mora \colon \setA \mtoin\SetL \setB$ and $\morb \colon \setC \mtoin\SetL \setD$, the function $\mora \mtimescatmor \morb$ maps $\maketupconcat{\ela, \elc}$ to $\maketupconcat{\mora(\ela), \morb(\elc)}$;
        \item the monoidal unit is the set $\idmoncat \definedas \emptycobj = \makeset{\tup{}}$, whose only element is the empty tuple.
    \end{itemize}
\end{lemma}

Let $\Pre$ denote the category whose objects are \SY{preorders} and whose morphisms are \SY{monotone functions}.
We define a category analogous to~\SetL, whose objects are ``tuple preorders''.

Given \SY{preorders}~$\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n$, where $n \setin \natnumbers$, we define the preorder
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:posetoftuples}
    \cObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n}\definedas \tup{\cObj{\posAsetn{1},\ldots, \posAsetn{n}}, \posleqof{\cObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n}}},
\end{equation}
where~$\cObj{\posAsetn{1},\ldots, \posAsetn{n}}$ is the set of tuples \cref{eq:sets-of-tuples}, and we use the product order:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:tuple-preorder-order}
    \prfdoubleperiod{
        \tup{\posela_1,\ldots, \posela_n}
        \posleqof{\cObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n}}
        \tup{\poselb_1,\ldots, \poselb_n}
    }{
        \posela_i \posleqof{\posA_i} \poselb_i \text{ for all } i\setin \makeset{1,\ldots, n}
    }
\end{equation}
This relation is reflexive and transitive because each of the relations $\posleqof{\posA_i}$ is, so $\cObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n}$ is indeed a preorder.
In the case $n = 0$, it is the preorder $\emptycobj$ whose only element is the empty tuple.

\begin{definition}[The category~\PreL]
    \SYNDEF{category of tuple-preorders and monotone functions}
    \label{def:PreL}
    The category~\PreL is the \SY{subcategory} of $\Pre$ where the objects are tuple preorders, that is, \SY{preorders} of the form
    \begin{equation}
        \cObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n} = \tup{\cObj{\posAsetn{1},\ldots, \posAsetn{n}}, \posleqof{\cObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n}}},
    \end{equation}
    and where the morphisms are all monotone functions between such preorders.
    % \begin{enumerate}
    %     \item \emph{Objects:} \SY{posets} of the form presented in \cref{eq:posetoftuples}.
    %     \item \emph{Morphisms:}
    %           Monotone functions between \SY{posets} of the form presented in \cref{eq:posetoftuples}.
    %     \item \emph{Composition:}
    %           Composition is the usual composition of functions.
    %     \item \emph{Identities:}
    %           Identity functions.
    % \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:PosL-associative-stacking}
    \label{lem:PreL-strict-monoidal}
    The following defines a strict monoidal structure on $\PreL$:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item on objects, the monoidal product concatenates the lists of preorders,
              \begin{equation}
                  \label{eq:multposstack}
                  \cObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_m} \mtimescatob \cObj{\posB_1,\ldots, \posB_n} \definedas
                  \cObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_m, \posB_1,\ldots, \posB_n};
              \end{equation}
              so the underlying set of a monoidal product is the product $\cprod$ of the underlying sets (\cref{eq:strict-monoidal-product-sets}), and $\maketupconcat{\posela, \poselb}$ is below $\maketupconcat{\posela', \poselb'}$ precisely when $\posela$ is below $\posela'$ and $\poselb$ is below $\poselb'$;
        \item on morphisms, the monoidal product is defined as in $\SetL$ (\cref{lem:SetL-strict-monoidal}): for monotone functions $\mora \colon \posA \mtoin\PreL \posB$ and $\morb \colon \posC \mtoin\PreL \posD$, the function $\mora \mtimescatmor \morb \colon \posA \mtimescatob \posC \mtoin\PreL \posB \mtimescatob \posD$ maps $\maketupconcat{\posela, \poselc}$ to $\maketupconcat{\mora(\posela), \morb(\poselc)}$;
        \item the monoidal unit is the preorder $\idmoncat \definedas \emptycobj$, whose only element is the empty tuple.
    \end{itemize}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    The function $\mora \mtimescatmor \morb$ is monotone because $\mora$ and $\morb$ are, and because the order of a monoidal product is defined entry by entry.
    All the conditions in \cref{def:strict-monoidal-cat} then follow from the corresponding statements for $\SetL$ (\cref{lem:SetL-strict-monoidal}).
    On the level of morphisms, this is because two monotone functions with the same source and target are equal as soon as their underlying functions are equal.
    On the level of objects, the underlying sets on the two sides of each condition are equal, and so are the orders, since by \cref{eq:tuple-preorder-order} two tuples are always compared entry by entry, however the list of preorders was put together.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The category~\PosL]
    \SYNDEF{category of tuple-posets and monotone maps}
    \label{def:PosL}
    If the preorders $\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n$ are \SY{posets}, then the tuple preorder $\cObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n}$ is a poset as well: if two tuples are below each other, then so are their entries, which are therefore equal.
    We write $\PosL$ for the \SY{full subcategory} of $\PreL$ whose objects are the tuple preorders which are posets.
    It is also a subcategory of the category $\Pos$ of posets and monotone functions.

    A monoidal product of two posets in $\PreL$ is again a poset, and the monoidal unit $\emptycobj$ is a poset.
    Hence the strict monoidal structure of \cref{lem:PreL-strict-monoidal} restricts to $\PosL$, which is thus a strict monoidal category as well.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Analogously:~\RelL, $\mRelL$, $\ProfBoolL$}
\phantomsection\label{def:RelL}\label{def:mRelL}\label{def:ProfBoolL}%
In a manner similar to the above examples, we can define a category~\RelL where the objects are sets of tuples (as in~\SetL) and the morphisms are relations, we can define a category $\mRelL$ where the objects are preorders of tuples (as in~\PreL) and the morphisms are monotone relations, and we can define a category $\ProfBoolL$ of Boolean profunctors between preorders of tuples.



    The categories $\RelL$ and $\mRelL$ can be equipped with strict monoidal structures in the same way as $\SetL$ and $\PreL$.
    On objects, the monoidal products and the monoidal units are those of $\SetL$ and of $\PreL$, respectively.
    On morphisms, the monoidal product of two relations $\relA \colon \setA \mto \setB$ and $\relB \colon \setC \mto \setD$ is the relation $\relA \mtimescatmor \relB \colon \setA \cprod \setC \mto \setB \cprod \setD$ which relates concatenated tuples entry by entry:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:RelL-monoidal-product}
        \relA \mtimescatmor \relB
        \definedas
        \makeset{
            \tup{\maketupconcat{\ela, \elc}, \maketupconcat{\elb, \eld}}
            \mid
            \tup{\ela, \elb} \setin \relA \text{ and } \tup{\elc, \eld} \setin \relB
        }.
    \end{equation}
    The same formula is used for monotone relations; it is the product of monotone relations (\cref{def:monrel-product}), with tuples concatenated instead of paired.


For $\ProfBoolL$, we spell out the resulting strict monoidal structure.

\subsection{Examples where the monoidal product is like a sum}
\label{sec:strict-monoidal-sums}

The \SY{cartesian product} is not the only way of putting two sets side by side.
The other basic one is their sum (\cref{def:disjoint-union-of-sets}): an element of $\setA \setdisunion \setB$ is \emph{either} an element of $\setA$ \emph{or} an element of $\setB$, together with an index which records which of the two it is.
In terms of wires, this is a different kind of parallel composition.
With a product, something travels along every wire.
With a sum, the wires are alternatives, and something travels along exactly one of them: think of a component which receives either a measurement or an error message, or of a machine which has several modes of operation.

Like the \SY{cartesian product}, the sum of sets is associative and unital only ``up to renaming'', and not on the nose.
In this subsection we repair this by a construction which mirrors the one of $\SetL$, and we then apply the same idea to relations and to preorders.
In all of these examples, an element of a monoidal product of two objects is an element of the first object \emph{or} an element of the second one.

\paragraph{The category \SetR}
\label{sec:cocartcatset}

We would like a strict monoidal structure on $\Set$ which is modeled on the sum of sets, with the empty set as its monoidal unit.
The sum itself does not provide one.
Recall that $\setA \setdisunion \setB = (\makeset{1} \cartprod \setA) \setunion (\makeset{2} \cartprod \setB)$.
For three sets $\setA$, $\setB$, and $\setC$, the elements of $(\setA \setdisunion \setB) \setdisunion \setC$ are of the form
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sum-assoc-fails-1}
    \tup{1, \tup{1, \ela}}, \qquad \tup{1, \tup{2, \elb}}, \qquad \tup{2, \elc},
\end{equation}
while the elements of $\setA \setdisunion (\setB \setdisunion \setC)$ are of the form
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sum-assoc-fails-2}
    \tup{1, \ela}, \qquad \tup{2, \tup{1, \elb}}, \qquad \tup{2, \tup{2, \elc}},
\end{equation}
with $\ela \setin \setA$, $\elb \setin \setB$, and $\elc \setin \setC$.
The two sets are isomorphic, but they are not equal: the indices are nested differently.
Similarly, the empty set is not neutral on the nose, since $\Emptyset \setdisunion \setB = \makeset{2} \cartprod \setB$ is not equal to $\setB$ (\cref{sec:disjoint-union}).

The remedy is the same as for the \SY{cartesian product}.
Instead of forming the sum of $n$ sets by adding two sets at a time, which leads to nested indices, we form it in one go, with a single index for each element: a number between $1$ and $n$, which says from which of the $n$ sets the element comes.
This is the $n$-ary sum of \cref{def:n-ary-disjoint-union-of-sets}.
It is instructive to compare the elements of the two constructions.
An element of a set of tuples $\cObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{n}}$ has an entry for \emph{every} one of the positions $1, \dots, n$.
An element of a sum of $n$ sets consists of \emph{one} of the positions $1, \dots, n$, together with an entry for this position only.

Concretely, we will define a category~\SetR whose objects are ``sets of indexed elements''.

Given non-empty sets~$\setAn{1}, \setAn{2}, \ldots, \setAn{n}$, where $n \setin \natnumbers$, we define
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sets-of-indexed-elements}
    \sObj{\setAn{1}, \setAn{2}, \ldots, \setAn{n}} \definedas \makeset{ \tup{i, \ela} \mid i \setin \makeset{1, 2, \ldots, n} \text{ and } \ela \setin \setAn{i} }.
\end{equation}
We call the number $i$ the \emph{index} of the element $\tup{i, \ela}$.
So for example,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:setR-example}
    \sObj{\wnumbers, \natnumbers, \reals} = \makeset{ \tup{1, \ela} \mid \ela \setin \wnumbers } \setunion \makeset{ \tup{2, \elb} \mid \elb \setin \natnumbers } \setunion \makeset{ \tup{3, \elc} \mid \elc \setin \reals }.
\end{equation}
The number $5$, for instance, gives rise to three different elements of this set, namely $\tup{1, 5}$, $\tup{2, 5}$, and $\tup{3, 5}$: the indices keep the three copies apart.

Note the case $n = 0$, where
\begin{equation}
    \sObj{} = \Emptyset
\end{equation}
is the empty set: there are no indices, and hence there are no elements.

We ask the sets $\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{n}$ to be non-empty for the following reason.
If $\setX = \sObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{n}}$ for non-empty sets $\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{n}$, then the indices which occur in $\setX$ are precisely the numbers $1, \dots, n$.
So we can read off from the set $\setX$ alone both the number $n$, which we call the \emph{width} of $\setX$ and denote by $\indexwidth{\setX}$, and the sets
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:indexed-elements-summands}
    \setAn{i} = \makeset{ \ela \mid \tup{i, \ela} \setin \setX }.
\end{equation}
In other words, a set of indexed elements determines the list of sets that it was built from.
This would no longer be true if we allowed empty sets.
An empty set contributes no elements, and so, for example, the list consisting of $\setA$ and $\Emptyset$ would give the same set $\makeset{1} \cartprod \setA$ as the list consisting of $\setA$ alone, although the first list has length two and the second one has length one.
We will need the width in a moment, and so we do not allow empty sets in our lists.
Nothing is lost by this: an alternative which can never occur is not much of an alternative.

All the sets of indexed elements are in particular just sets, so we can package them into a category whose morphisms are simply functions between such sets.

\begin{ctdefinition}[\SetR]\label{def:SetR}
    The category \SetR is the category whose objects are those sets which are of the form~$\sObj{\setAn{1}, \setAn{2}, \ldots, \setAn{n}}$, for any~$n \setin \natnumbers$ and any non-empty sets~$\setAn{1}, \setAn{2}, \ldots, \setAn{n}$.
    Morphisms in \SetR are any functions between such sets, and the composition operations and identities are the usual ones for functions.
\end{ctdefinition}

\paragraph{From \Set to~\SetR}

Observe that we can turn any set into an object of \SetR: given a set~\setA, there is the associated set
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:setintosetr}
    \makeset{1} \cartprod \setA = \makeset{ \tup{1, \ela} \mid \ela \setin \setA }
\end{equation}
whose elements are the elements of~\setA, each one with the index $1$.
If~\setA is non-empty, this is the object $\sObj{\setA}$, of width one; if~\setA is empty, it is the object $\emptysobj = \Emptyset$, of width zero.
This defines a function
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:setintosetrfunction}
    \begin{aligned}
        \Obof{\Set} & \to \Obof{\SetR} \\
        \setA       & \mapsto \makeset{1} \cartprod \setA
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
which one might call ``bracket'', as for \SetL.

Similarly, given a function~$\mapa \colon \setA \sto \setB$ between sets, there is an associated function~$\sMor{ \mapa }\colon \makeset{1} \cartprod \setA \mto \makeset{1} \cartprod \setB$ given by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:functionintosetrmor}
    \sMor{ \mapa } ( \tup{1, \ela }) \definedas \tup{1, \mapa(\ela)}.
\end{equation}
This defines a function
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:functionintosetrmorfun}
    \Hom_{\Set}(\setA,\setB) \to \Hom_{\SetR}(\makeset{1} \cartprod \setA, \makeset{1} \cartprod \setB)
\end{equation}
for any sets~\setA and~\setB.

\paragraph{Shifting indices}

Given an indexed element $\tup{j, \elb}$ and a natural number $m$, we can \emph{shift} its index by $m$.
We write
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:index-shift-element}
    \indexshift{\tup{j, \elb}}{m} \definedas \tup{m + j, \elb}
\end{equation}
for the resulting indexed element.
Given a set $\setB$ of indexed elements, we write
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:index-shift-set}
    \indexshift{\setB}{m} \definedas \makeset{ \tup{m + j, \elb} \mid \tup{j, \elb} \setin \setB }
\end{equation}
for the set which is obtained by shifting the indices of all of its elements by $m$.
For example, the indices which occur in $\indexshift{\sObj{\setBn{1}, \setBn{2}}}{3}$ are $4$ and $5$.

Shifting by $0$ changes nothing, and shifting by $m$ and then by $l$ is the same as shifting by $l + m$.
These two facts, which express that addition of natural numbers has the neutral element $0$ and is \SY{associative}, will play the role which the concatenation of tuples played for~\SetL.

\paragraph{Multiplication in \SetR}
\label{sec:multiplication-for-SetR}
We define a multiplication for objects of \SetR.
The symbol~$\sprod$ will denote this multiplication in infix notation.

Given~$\sObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{m}}$ and~$\sObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{n}}$ we define the operation
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:mult-setr-sign}
    \sprod \colon \Obof{\SetR} \cartprod \Obof{\SetR} \to \Obof{\SetR}
\end{equation}
by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:strict-monoidal-sum-sets}
    \sObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{m}} \sprod \sObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{n}}
    \definedas \sObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{m}, \setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{n}}
\end{equation}
for any $n, m \setin \natnumbers$.
This is well-defined because, as we saw above, an object of \SetR determines the list of non-empty sets that it was built from.

In terms of elements, for any objects $\setX$ and $\setY$ of \SetR we have
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:strict-monoidal-sum-sets-elements}
    \setX \sprod \setY = \setX \setunion \indexshift{\setY}{\indexwidth{\setX}}.
\end{equation}
So the elements of~$\setX \sprod \setY$ are the elements of~$\setX$, unchanged, together with the elements of~$\setY$, whose indices have been shifted by the width of~$\setX$, so that they come after the indices of~$\setX$.
The two kinds of elements cannot be confused: an element of $\setX \sprod \setY$ belongs to $\setX$ if its index is at most $\indexwidth{\setX}$, and it belongs to the shifted copy of $\setY$ otherwise.
Note also that the widths add up: $\indexwidth{\setX \sprod \setY} = \indexwidth{\setX} + \indexwidth{\setY}$.

Equation \cref{eq:strict-monoidal-sum-sets} holds in particular also in the cases when~$m = 0$ or~$n =0$:
\begin{equation}
    \emptysobj \sprod \sObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{n}}
    =
    \sObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{n}}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \sObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{m}} \sprod \emptysobj
    =
    \sObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{m}}.
\end{equation}
In other words, the empty set is neutral for the multiplication on the nose: $\Emptyset \sprod \setY = \setY$ and $\setX \sprod \Emptyset = \setX$.

The multiplication of objects in~\SetR is \SY{associative}.
Indeed, both
\begin{equation}
    ( \sObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{l}} \sprod \sObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{m}} ) \sprod \sObj{\setCn{1}, \ldots, \setCn{n}}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \sObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{l}} \sprod ( \sObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{m}} \sprod \sObj{\setCn{1}, \ldots, \setCn{n}} )
\end{equation}
are equal to
\begin{equation}
    \sObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{l}, \setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{m}, \setCn{1}, \ldots, \setCn{n}}.
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}
    Note that, for any non-empty sets $\setA, \setB$, we have
    \begin{equation}
        \sObj{\setA, \setB} = \setA \setdisunion \setB,
    \end{equation}
    and, more generally, $\sObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{n}}$ is the $n$-ary sum of \cref{def:n-ary-disjoint-union-of-sets}.
    In other words, the multiplication operation in $\SetR$ ``coincides'' with the sum operation in the special case when we are adding two non-empty sets together.

    Because of this, the sum is well-defined as an operation on the non-empty objects of $\SetR$.
    Namely, given non-empty sets of indexed elements $\sObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{l}}$ and $\sObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{m}}$, their sum is again a set of indexed elements:
    \begin{equation}
        \sObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{l}} \setdisunion \sObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{m}} = \sObj{\sObj{\setAn{1}, \ldots, \setAn{l}}, \sObj{\setBn{1}, \ldots, \setBn{m}}},
    \end{equation}
    (which is a ``nested'' set of indexed elements, of width two).
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:SetR-strict-monoidal}
    The following defines a strict monoidal structure on $\SetR$:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item on objects, the monoidal product is the multiplication $\sprod$ of \cref{eq:strict-monoidal-sum-sets};
        \item on morphisms, the monoidal product is defined as follows: for $\mora \colon \setA \mtoin\SetR \setB$ and $\morb \colon \setC \mtoin\SetR \setD$, the function $\mora \mtimescatmor \morb \colon \setA \sprod \setC \mtoin\SetR \setB \sprod \setD$ is given by
              \begin{equation}
                  \label{eq:SetR-monoidal-product-morphisms}
                  \begin{aligned}
                      (\mora \mtimescatmor \morb)(\ela)                                  & \definedas \mora(\ela)                                  &  & \text{for } \ela \setin \setA, \\
                      (\mora \mtimescatmor \morb)(\indexshift{\elc}{\indexwidth{\setA}}) & \definedas \indexshift{\morb(\elc)}{\indexwidth{\setB}} &  & \text{for } \elc \setin \setC;
                  \end{aligned}
              \end{equation}
        \item the monoidal unit is the empty set, $\idmoncat \definedas \emptysobj = \Emptyset$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{lemma}

In words: an element of $\setA$ is mapped to an element of $\setB$ by $\mora$.
For an element of the shifted copy of $\setC$, we undo the shift, apply $\morb$, and shift the result by the width of $\setB$, so that it lands in the shifted copy of $\setD$.
Note that the two shifts are different in general: the first one is by the width of the source $\setA$ of $\mora$, and the second one is by the width of its target $\setB$.

\begin{proof}
    We check the conditions of \cref{def:strict-monoidal-cat}.
    First, $\mora \mtimescatmor \morb$ is a well-defined function, since every element of $\setA \sprod \setC$ is either an element of $\setA$, or it is obtained from a unique element of $\setC$ by a shift by $\indexwidth{\setA}$, and never both.

    The monoidal product is a functor.
    It maps identity functions to identity functions, since $\catidat{\setA} \mtimescatmor \catidat{\setC}$ maps $\ela$ to $\ela$ and $\indexshift{\elc}{\indexwidth{\setA}}$ to $\indexshift{\elc}{\indexwidth{\setA}}$.
    For the compatibility with composition, let also $\mora' \colon \setB \mtoin\SetR \setB'$ and $\morb' \colon \setD \mtoin\SetR \setD'$ be given.
    The function $\mora \mtimescatmor \morb$ maps elements of $\setA$ to elements of $\setB$, and shifted elements of $\setC$ to shifted elements of $\setD$.
    Hence $(\mora \mtimescatmor \morb) \mthen (\mora' \mtimescatmor \morb')$ maps $\ela$ to $\mora'(\mora(\ela))$, and it maps $\indexshift{\elc}{\indexwidth{\setA}}$ first to $\indexshift{\morb(\elc)}{\indexwidth{\setB}}$ and then to $\indexshift{\morb'(\morb(\elc))}{\indexwidth{\setB'}}$.
    This is what $(\mora \mthen \mora') \mtimescatmor (\morb \mthen \morb')$ does.

    The monoidal product is associative on objects, as we saw above.
    For morphisms, let $\mora \colon \setA \mtoin\SetR \setB$, $\morb \colon \setC \mtoin\SetR \setD$, and $\morc \colon \setE \mtoin\SetR \setF$ be given.
    The elements of $\setA \sprod \setC \sprod \setE$ are the elements $\ela$ of $\setA$, the elements $\elc$ of $\setC$, shifted by $\indexwidth{\setA}$, and the elements $\ele$ of $\setE$, shifted by $\indexwidth{\setA} + \indexwidth{\setC}$.
    Both $(\mora \mtimescatmor \morb) \mtimescatmor \morc$ and $\mora \mtimescatmor (\morb \mtimescatmor \morc)$ map
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
            \ela                                                 & \mapsto \mora(\ela), \\
            \indexshift{\elc}{\indexwidth{\setA}}                    & \mapsto \indexshift{\morb(\elc)}{\indexwidth{\setB}}, \\
            \indexshift{\ele}{\indexwidth{\setA} + \indexwidth{\setC}} & \mapsto \indexshift{\morc(\ele)}{\indexwidth{\setB} + \indexwidth{\setD}}.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    Here we use that the widths add up, and that shifting by $\indexwidth{\setC}$ and then by $\indexwidth{\setA}$ is the same as shifting by $\indexwidth{\setA} + \indexwidth{\setC}$.

    Finally, the monoidal unit is neutral on objects, as we saw above, and it is neutral on morphisms.
    Indeed, $\setA \sprod \Emptyset = \setA$, and $\mora \mtimescatmor \catidat{\Emptyset}$ maps every $\ela \setin \setA$ to $\mora(\ela)$.
    And $\Emptyset \sprod \setA = \setA$, where every element of $\setA$ is regarded as shifted by $\indexwidth{\Emptyset} = 0$, and $\catidat{\Emptyset} \mtimescatmor \mora$ maps $\ela = \indexshift{\ela}{0}$ to $\indexshift{\mora(\ela)}{0} = \mora(\ela)$.
\end{proof}

The monoidal product of morphisms in \SetR is a strict version of the sum of functions (\cref{def:sum-of-functions}): for functions $\mapa \colon \setA \sto \setB$ and $\mapb \colon \setC \sto \setD$ between non-empty sets, the function $\sMor{\mapa} \mtimescatmor \sMor{\mapb}$ is equal to the sum $\mapa \funcsum \mapb \colon \setA \setdisunion \setC \sto \setB \setdisunion \setD$.

\paragraph{The category~\RelR}
\label{sec:RelR}
\phantomsection\label{def:RelR}%
In the same way as we passed from $\SetL$ to $\RelL$, we can define a category~\RelR whose objects are the objects of~\SetR, that is, sets of indexed elements, and whose morphisms are relations, with the usual composition and identities of relations.

The category~\RelR can be equipped with a strict monoidal structure which is modeled on the sum.
On objects, the monoidal product and the monoidal unit are those of~\SetR.
On morphisms, the monoidal product of two relations $\relA \colon \setA \mto \setB$ and $\relB \colon \setC \mto \setD$ is the relation $\relA \mtimescatmor \relB \colon \setA \sprod \setC \mto \setB \sprod \setD$ given by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:RelR-monoidal-product}
    \relA \mtimescatmor \relB
    \definedas
    \relA \setunion
    \makeset{
        \tup{\indexshift{\elc}{\indexwidth{\setA}}, \indexshift{\eld}{\indexwidth{\setB}}}
        \mid
        \tup{\elc, \eld} \setin \relB
    }.
\end{equation}
So $\relA \mtimescatmor \relB$ relates the elements of $\setA$ to the elements of $\setB$ in the way $\relA$ does, it relates the shifted elements of $\setC$ to the shifted elements of $\setD$ in the way $\relB$ does, and it never relates an element of one of the two parts to an element of the other part.
Compare this with the monoidal product of $\RelL$ in \cref{eq:RelL-monoidal-product}: there, a pair of related elements consists of a pair of elements related by $\relA$ \emph{and} a pair of elements related by $\relB$; here, it is a pair of elements related by $\relA$ \emph{or} a (shifted) pair of elements related by $\relB$.

That this defines a strict monoidal structure is checked in the same way as for~\SetR in \cref{lem:SetR-strict-monoidal}.
For example, the monoidal product is compatible with composition because a composition of two relations of the form \cref{eq:RelR-monoidal-product} can only pass through elements of one and the same part, and the empty relation $\Emptyset \mto \Emptyset$, which is the identity morphism of the monoidal unit, is neutral because forming the union with an empty set changes nothing.
If $\relA$ and $\relB$ are functions, then so is $\relA \mtimescatmor \relB$, and it is the function defined in \cref{eq:SetR-monoidal-product-morphisms}; so the monoidal product of~\RelR extends the one of~\SetR.

\paragraph{The category~\PreR}
\label{sec:PreR}

Finally, we apply the same idea to preorders.
Recall that the sum of two preorders (\cref{def:disjoint-union-of-posets}) has the sum of the two underlying sets as its underlying set, and that two of its elements can only be related if they come from the same preorder.
We define a category analogous to~\SetR, whose objects are ``preorders of indexed elements''.

Given non-empty \SY{preorders}~$\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n$, where $n \setin \natnumbers$, we define the preorder
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:preorderofindexedelements}
    \sObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n}\definedas \tup{\sObj{\posAsetn{1},\ldots, \posAsetn{n}}, \posleqof{\sObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n}}},
\end{equation}
where~$\sObj{\posAsetn{1},\ldots, \posAsetn{n}}$ is the set of indexed elements \cref{eq:sets-of-indexed-elements}, and where two elements are compared only if they have the same index:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:indexed-preorder-order}
    \prfdoubleperiod{
        \tup{i, \posela}
        \posleqof{\sObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n}}
        \tup{j, \poselb}
    }{
        i = j \text{ and } \posela \posleqof{\posA_i} \poselb
    }
\end{equation}
This relation is reflexive and transitive because each of the relations $\posleqof{\posA_i}$ is (compare \cref{lem:sum-preorder}), so $\sObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n}$ is indeed a preorder.
In the case $n = 0$, it is the empty preorder $\emptysobj$, and in the case $n = 2$, it is the sum $\posA_1 \Pplus \posA_2$ of \cref{def:disjoint-union-of-posets}.

\begin{definition}[The category~\PreR]
    \label{def:PreR}
    The category~\PreR is the \SY{subcategory} of $\Pre$ where the objects are preorders of indexed elements, that is, \SY{preorders} of the form
    \begin{equation}
        \sObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n} = \tup{\sObj{\posAsetn{1},\ldots, \posAsetn{n}}, \posleqof{\sObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n}}},
    \end{equation}
    for any $n \setin \natnumbers$ and any non-empty preorders $\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n$, and where the morphisms are all monotone functions between such preorders.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:PreR-strict-monoidal}
    The following defines a strict monoidal structure on $\PreR$:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item on objects, the monoidal product concatenates the lists of preorders,
              \begin{equation}
                  \label{eq:multpresum}
                  \sObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_m} \mtimescatob \sObj{\posB_1,\ldots, \posB_n} \definedas
                  \sObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_m, \posB_1,\ldots, \posB_n};
              \end{equation}
              so the underlying set of a monoidal product is the product $\sprod$ of the underlying sets (\cref{eq:strict-monoidal-sum-sets}), two elements of the first preorder are compared as before, and so are two shifted elements of the second preorder, while an element of the first preorder is never related to a shifted element of the second one;
        \item on morphisms, the monoidal product is defined as in \cref{lem:SetR-strict-monoidal}: for monotone functions $\mora \colon \posA \mtoin\PreR \posB$ and $\morb \colon \posC \mtoin\PreR \posD$, the function $\mora \mtimescatmor \morb$ from $\posA \mtimescatob \posC$ to $\posB \mtimescatob \posD$ is given by \cref{eq:SetR-monoidal-product-morphisms};
        \item the monoidal unit is the empty preorder, $\idmoncat \definedas \emptysobj$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    The function $\mora \mtimescatmor \morb$ is monotone.
    Indeed, if one element of $\posA \mtimescatob \posC$ is below another one, then they are either both elements of $\posA$, or both shifted elements of $\posC$.
    In the first case, their images are compared as their images under $\mora$ are, and in the second case as their images under $\morb$ are, since shifting the indices of two elements by the same number does not affect how they are compared.
    So $\mora \mtimescatmor \morb$ is monotone because $\mora$ and $\morb$ are.

    All the conditions in \cref{def:strict-monoidal-cat} then follow from the corresponding statements for $\SetR$ (\cref{lem:SetR-strict-monoidal}), as in the proof of \cref{lem:PreL-strict-monoidal}.
    On the level of morphisms, this is because two monotone functions with the same source and target are equal as soon as their underlying functions are equal.
    On the level of objects, the underlying sets on the two sides of each condition are equal, and so are the orders, since by \cref{eq:indexed-preorder-order} two elements are always compared within the preorder that they come from, however the list of preorders was put together.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The category~\PosR]
    \label{def:PosR}
    A preorder of indexed elements $\sObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n}$ is a \SY{poset} precisely when all of the preorders $\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n$ are posets.
    We write $\PosR$ for the \SY{full subcategory} of $\PreR$ whose objects are the preorders of indexed elements which are posets.
    It is also a subcategory of the category $\Pos$ of posets and monotone functions.

    A monoidal product of two posets in $\PreR$ is again a poset, and the monoidal unit, the empty preorder, is a poset.
    Hence the strict monoidal structure of \cref{lem:PreR-strict-monoidal} restricts to $\PosR$, which is thus a strict monoidal category as well.
    It is a strict version of the category $\Pos$ with the sum of posets, just as $\PosL$ is a strict version of $\Pos$ with the product of posets.
\end{remark}


\subsection{Applied examples}

\begin{example}[Codesign]
    \label{exa:codesign-strict-monoidal}
    Recall from \cref{chap:application-design-problems} that in codesign we model a component of an engineering system as a relationship between the functionalities that it provides and the requirements that it has.
    Formally, such a component is a \emph{feasibility relation} $\monrelM \colon \funsp \profto \ressp$ between a poset $\funsp$ of functionalities and a poset $\ressp$ of requirements (\cref{def:feasibility-relation}), or, equivalently, a \SY{design problem} $\adp \colon \funspop \Ptimes \ressp \mto \Bool$ (\cref{def:design-problem}), which is a Boolean profunctor between posets (\cref{def:boolean-profunctor}).
    We have also seen that components can be connected in series, and that they are the morphisms of two categories which describe the same thing in two ways: the category $\feas$ of feasibility relations (\cref{def:Feas}) and the category $\DP$ of design problems (\cref{def:DP}).

    In the terminology of this chapter, $\feas$ is the \SY{full subcategory} of the category $\mRel$ of monotone relations, and $\DP$ is the \SY{full subcategory} of the category $\ProfBool$ of Boolean profunctors, in which we only consider those objects which are posets.

    To model the parallel composition of components, we can use the tuple versions of these two categories instead.
    \SYNDEF{category of tuple-posets and design problems}%
    \label{def:DPL}%
    We write $\feasL$ for the \SY{full subcategory} of $\mRelL$, and $\DPL$ for the \SY{full subcategory} of $\ProfBoolL$, in which we only consider those objects which are tuple preorders that are posets, that is, the objects of $\PosL$.
    These are also subcategories of $\feas$ and of $\DP$, respectively: the morphisms of $\feasL$ are the feasibility relations, and the morphisms of $\DPL$ are the design problems, between posets of tuples.
    An object $\cObj{\posA_1,\ldots, \posA_n}$ then stands for a list of $n$ functionalities, or of $n$ requirements, each with its own poset of possible values.

    The monoidal product of two posets of tuples is a poset, and so the strict monoidal structures of $\mRelL$ and of $\ProfBoolL$ (\cref{eq:RelL-monoidal-product} and \cref{lem:ProfBoolL-strict-monoidal}) restrict to $\feasL$ and to $\DPL$.
    The monoidal product of two components is the component which provides the functionalities of both, listed one after the other, and which has the requirements of both; by \cref{eq:dpmulti}, a pair of lists of functionalities is feasible with a pair of lists of requirements precisely when this is the case for each of the two components separately.
    In this way, placing two components of a system side by side, without any interaction between them, is modeled by the monoidal product of the strict monoidal categories $\feasL$ and $\DPL$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Mealy machines]
    \label{exa:MealyModL-strict-monoidal}
    Recall the category $\MealyMod$ of Mealy machines up to isomorphism (\cref{def:MealyCat}).
    Two Mealy machines can be run side by side: the combined machine receives a pair of inputs, one for each machine, each machine updates its own state, and the combined machine returns the pair of outputs.
    We now turn this into a strict monoidal structure.

    For this, we need to be careful with the sets involved.
    In $\MealyMod$, the sets of inputs, states, and outputs are arbitrary sets, and they are combined using the \SY{cartesian product}, which is not associative on the nose.
    As for $\SetL$ (\cref{lem:SetL-strict-monoidal}), we therefore let all three kinds of sets live in $\SetL$, and we combine them using the multiplication $\cprod$ of $\SetL$.

    We define the category $\MealyModL$ as follows.
    Its objects are the objects of $\SetL$.
    A morphism from $\prin$ to $\prout$ is an equivalence class $\equivmora$ of Mealy machines
    \begin{equation}
        \mora = \tupp{\prin, \prst, \prout, \prdyn, \prreadout},
    \end{equation}
    in which the set $\prin$ of inputs, the set $\prst$ of states, and the set $\prout$ of outputs are all objects of $\SetL$, the dynamics and the read-out are morphisms
    \begin{equation}
        \prdyn \colon \prin \cprod \prst \mtoin\SetL \prst
        \qqand
        \prreadout \colon \prin \cprod \prst \mtoin\SetL \prout
    \end{equation}
    of $\SetL$.
    As before, two such machines with the same inputs and outputs are equivalent if they are isomorphic, that is, if there is a bijection between their sets of states which is compatible with the dynamics and the read-outs.

    Composition is defined as in $\MealyMod$ (\cref{eq:mealy-composition}), with $\cprod$ in place of the \SY{cartesian product}: given Mealy machines $\mora$ and $\morb$ with $\prout_{\mora} = \prin_{\morb}$, the machine $\morab$ has the set of states $\prst_{\mora} \cprod \prst_{\morb}$, and
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:mealyL-composition}
        \begin{aligned}
            \prdyn_{\morab}\bigl(\maketupconcat{\prinel, \prsteln{\mora}, \prsteln{\morb}}\bigr) & \definedas \maketupconcat{\prdyn_{\mora}(\maketupconcat{\prinel, \prsteln{\mora}}),\ \prdyn_{\morb}\bigl(\maketupconcat{\prreadout_{\mora}(\maketupconcat{\prinel, \prsteln{\mora}}), \prsteln{\morb}}\bigr)}, \\
            \prreadout_{\morab}\bigl(\maketupconcat{\prinel, \prsteln{\mora}, \prsteln{\morb}}\bigr) & \definedas \prreadout_{\morb}\bigl(\maketupconcat{\prreadout_{\mora}(\maketupconcat{\prinel, \prsteln{\mora}}), \prsteln{\morb}}\bigr).
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    The identity morphism of $\prin$ is represented by the machine with the set of states $\emptycobj$, whose read-out returns its input.
    (Every set of states is in bijection with an object of $\SetL$, namely with the set of tuples of length one over it. Hence $\MealyModL$ can be identified with the \SY{full subcategory} of $\MealyMod$ whose objects are the objects of $\SetL$.)

    The following defines a strict monoidal structure on $\MealyModL$.

    On objects, the monoidal product is the one of $\SetL$, that is, $\prin \mtimescatob \prin' \definedas \prin \cprod \prin'$, and the monoidal unit is $\idmoncat \definedas \emptycobj$.

    On morphisms, given morphisms $\equivmora$ and $\equivmorb$ represented by Mealy machines
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
            \mora & = \tupp{\prin_{\mora},\prst_{\mora},\prout_{\mora},\prdyn_{\mora},\prreadout_{\mora}}, \\
            \morb & = \tupp{\prin_{\morb},\prst_{\morb},\prout_{\morb},\prdyn_{\morb},\prreadout_{\morb}},
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    we define $\equivmora \mtimescatmor \equivmorb$ to be the equivalence class of the Mealy machine
    \begin{equation}
        \mora \mtimescatmor \morb \definedas \tupp{\prin_{\mora} \cprod \prin_{\morb},\ \prst_{\mora} \cprod \prst_{\morb},\ \prout_{\mora} \cprod \prout_{\morb},\ \prdyn,\ \prreadout},
    \end{equation}
    whose dynamics and read-out are
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:mealy-stacking}
        \begin{aligned}
            \prdyn\bigl(\maketupconcat{\prineln{\mora},\prineln{\morb},\prsteln{\mora}, \prsteln{\morb}}\bigr) & \definedas \maketupconcat{\prdyn_{\mora}(\maketupconcat{\prineln{\mora}, \prsteln{\mora}}),\ \prdyn_{\morb}(\maketupconcat{\prineln{\morb}, \prsteln{\morb}})}, \\
            \prreadout\bigl(\maketupconcat{\prineln{\mora},\prineln{\morb},\prsteln{\mora}, \prsteln{\morb}}\bigr) & \definedas \maketupconcat{\prreadout_{\mora}(\maketupconcat{\prineln{\mora}, \prsteln{\mora}}),\ \prreadout_{\morb}(\maketupconcat{\prineln{\morb}, \prsteln{\morb}})}.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    This does not depend on the choice of representatives: if $\mapc_1$ is an isomorphism of Mealy machines from $\mora$ to $\mora'$, and $\mapc_2$ one from $\morb$ to $\morb'$, then $\maketupconcat{\prsteln{\mora}, \prsteln{\morb}} \mapsto \maketupconcat{\mapc_1(\prsteln{\mora}), \mapc_2(\prsteln{\morb})}$ is one from $\mora \mtimescatmor \morb$ to $\mora' \mtimescatmor \morb'$.

    Let us go through the conditions of \cref{def:strict-monoidal-cat}.
    Since the sets of states now live in $\SetL$ as well, most of them hold on the nose, already for the machines themselves:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Associativity: the machines $(\mora \mtimescatmor \morb) \mtimescatmor \morc$ and $\mora \mtimescatmor (\morb \mtimescatmor \morc)$ are equal.
              In particular, they have the same set of states, since $(\prst_{\mora} \cprod \prst_{\morb}) \cprod \prst_{\morc} = \prst_{\mora} \cprod (\prst_{\morb} \cprod \prst_{\morc})$.
        \item Neutrality of the unit: the machine $\mora \mtimescatmor \catid_\idmoncat$ is equal to $\mora$, since $\prst_{\mora} \cprod \emptycobj = \prst_{\mora}$; similarly for $\catid_\idmoncat \mtimescatmor \mora$.
        \item Functoriality: stacking two identity machines gives an identity machine, since $\emptycobj \cprod \emptycobj = \emptycobj$.
              However, the machines $(\mora \mthen \morc) \mtimescatmor (\morb \mthen \mord)$ and $(\mora \mtimescatmor \morb) \mthen (\morc \mtimescatmor \mord)$ are \emph{not} equal: their sets of states are
              \begin{equation}
                  \label{eq:mealyL-interchange-states}
                  \prst_{\mora} \cprod \prst_{\morc} \cprod \prst_{\morb} \cprod \prst_{\mord}
                  \qqand
                  \prst_{\mora} \cprod \prst_{\morb} \cprod \prst_{\morc} \cprod \prst_{\mord},
              \end{equation}
              respectively, in which the states of $\morb$ and of $\morc$ appear in the opposite order.
              The bijection which exchanges the two middle components is an isomorphism of Mealy machines between them, and so the two machines represent the same morphism of $\MealyModL$.
    \end{itemize}
    So the only condition for which we really need to pass to equivalence classes is the compatibility of the monoidal product with composition.
    We will meet exactly the same phenomenon for LTI systems in \cref{exa:LTI-stacking-not-functorial} and \cref{exa:LTI-quotient-strict-monoidal}.
    (For the same reason, composition in $\MealyModL$ is associative and unital already for the machines themselves.)
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Stacking of LTI systems is not functorial]
    \label{exa:LTI-stacking-not-functorial}
    Consider the category \LTI with the stacking operations described in \cref{eq:LTI-stacking-objects} and \cref{eq:LTI-stacking-prftree}.
    At first sight this looks like a strict monoidal category.

    On the side of objects, the stacking $\reals^l \mtimescatob \reals^m = \reals^{l+m}$ is associative on the nose, and $\reals^{l+0}=\reals^{0+l}=\reals^l$ for any object $\reals^l\setin \Ob_\LTI$, so the object $\reals^0$ is a candidate for the monoidal unit.
    On the side of morphisms, stacking is associative on the nose as well, since it is given by block-diagonal matrices.
    The identity morphism of $\reals^0$ is the LTI system
    \begin{equation*}
        \catid_\idmoncat=\tupp{\reals^0, \mat{0}^{0\times 0}, \mat{0}^{0\times 0}, \mat{0}^{0\times 0},\mat{0}^{0\times 0}},
    \end{equation*}
    whose state space is $\reals^0$, in accordance with \cref{def:LTICat}, and it is neutral for stacking.
    Indeed, consider a morphism $\mora = \tupp{\reals^k, \mat{A}, \mat{B}, \mat{C}, \mat{D}} \colon \reals^l\mto \reals^m$, with state space $\reals^k$.
    Using $\reals^{k+0} = \reals^k$, we have:
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{aligned}
            \mora \mtimescatmor \catid_\idmoncat & =
            \langle \reals^{k+0},
            \begin{bmatrix}\mat{A}&\mat{0}\\ \mat{0}&\mat{0}^{0\times 0}\end{bmatrix},
            \begin{bmatrix}\mat{B}&\mat{0}\\ \mat{0}&\mat{0}^{0\times 0}\end{bmatrix},
            \begin{bmatrix}\mat{C}&\mat{0}\\ \mat{0}&\mat{0}^{0\times 0}\end{bmatrix},
            \begin{bmatrix}\mat{D}&\mat{0}\\ \mat{0}&\mat{0}^{0\times 0}\end{bmatrix}\rangle \\
                                                 & =\tupp{\reals^k, \mat{A}, \mat{B}, \mat{C}, \mat{D}} = \mora.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}
    Similarly, using $\reals^{0+k} = \reals^k$:
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{aligned}
            \catid_\idmoncat \mtimescatmor \mora & =
            \langle \reals^{0+k},
            \begin{bmatrix}\mat{0}^{0\times 0}&\mat{0}\\\mat{0}&\mat{A}\end{bmatrix},
            \begin{bmatrix}\mat{0}^{0\times 0}&\mat{0}\\\mat{0}&\mat{B}\end{bmatrix},
            \begin{bmatrix}\mat{0}^{0\times 0}&\mat{0}\\\mat{0}&\mat{C}\end{bmatrix},
            \begin{bmatrix}\mat{0}^{0\times 0}&\mat{0}\\\mat{0}&\mat{D}\end{bmatrix}\rangle \\
                                                 & =\tupp{\reals^k, \mat{A}, \mat{B}, \mat{C}, \mat{D}} = \mora.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}

    Nevertheless, \LTI with these operations is \emph{not} a \SY{strict monoidal category} in the sense of \cref{def:strict-monoidal-cat}, because the stacking operations are not the components of a functor: the first condition in \cref{def:functorial-stacking-cat} fails.
    To see this, consider LTI systems $\mora$, $\morb$, $\morc$, and $\mord$ such that $\mora \mthen \morc$ and $\morb \mthen \mord$ are defined, and compare the two LTI systems
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:LTI-interchange-two-sides}
        (\mora \mthen \morc) \mtimescatmor (\morb \mthen \mord)
        \qqand
        (\mora \mtimescatmor \morb) \mthen (\morc \mtimescatmor \mord).
    \end{equation}
    Both series composition (\cref{eq:LTI-composition}) and stacking (\cref{eq:LTI-stacking-mor1}) build the state of the resulting system by stacking the states of the two given systems on top of each other, with the first system on top.
    Hence, writing $\mat{x}_\mora$, $\mat{x}_\morb$, $\mat{x}_\morc$, and $\mat{x}_\mord$ for states of the four given systems, the states of the two systems in \cref{eq:LTI-interchange-two-sides} are the vectors
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:LTI-interchange-states}
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \mat{x}_\mora \\
            \mat{x}_\morc \\
            \mat{x}_\morb \\
            \mat{x}_\mord
        \end{bmatrix}
        \qqand
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \mat{x}_\mora \\
            \mat{x}_\morb \\
            \mat{x}_\morc \\
            \mat{x}_\mord
        \end{bmatrix},
    \end{equation}
    respectively: the coordinates belonging to $\morb$ and to $\morc$ appear in the opposite order.
    Consequently, the blocks of the matrices which belong to $\morb$ and to $\morc$ appear in the opposite order in the two systems as well.
    For instance, by \cref{eq:LTI-composition} and \cref{eq:LTI-stacking-mor2}, the matrices $\mat{C}$ of the two systems are
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:LTI-interchange-C-matrices}
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \mat{D}_\morc\mat{C}_\mora & \mat{C}_\morc & \mat{0}                    & \mat{0}       \\
            \mat{0}                    & \mat{0}       & \mat{D}_\mord\mat{C}_\morb & \mat{C}_\mord
        \end{bmatrix}
        \qqand
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \mat{D}_\morc\mat{C}_\mora & \mat{0}                    & \mat{C}_\morc & \mat{0}       \\
            \mat{0}                    & \mat{D}_\mord\mat{C}_\morb & \mat{0}       & \mat{C}_\mord
        \end{bmatrix},
    \end{equation}
    respectively.
    These are different matrices in general: for instance, if all four state spaces are $\reals^1$, they differ as soon as $\mat{C}_\morc \neq \mat{0}$.
    (Computing the two matrices $\mat{A}$ is the subject of \linkifexists{ex:StackingLTI}.)

    Note that the two systems in \cref{eq:LTI-interchange-two-sides} are ``essentially the same'': they have the same four subsystems, connected in the same way, and they only list the coordinates of their state in a different order.
    But as morphisms of \LTI they are not equal.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[LTI systems up to equivalence]
    \label{exa:LTI-quotient-strict-monoidal}
    The failure of functoriality in \cref{exa:LTI-stacking-not-functorial} can be repaired in the same way in which we obtained the categories of Moore and Mealy machines (\cref{sec:cat-machines}): by no longer distinguishing systems which only differ by a change of coordinates of their state space.

    Recall from \cref{def:equivalence_lti} that two LTI systems $\mora$ and $\mora'$ with the same input space, the same output space, and state spaces of the same dimension $k$ are \emph{equivalent}, written $\mora \equival \mora'$, if there is an invertible matrix $\mat{T} \setin \reals^{\matdim{k}{k}}$ such that
    \begin{equation}
        \mat{A}_{\mora'} = \mat{T}\mat{A}_\mora \mat{T}^{-1}, \quad \mat{B}_{\mora'} = \mat{T}\mat{B}_\mora, \quad \mat{C}_{\mora'} =\mat{C}_\mora\mat{T}^{-1},\quad \mat{D}_{\mora'}=\mat{D}_\mora.
    \end{equation}
    Equivalent systems are the same system, described in different coordinates of the state space: started in states which correspond to each other under $\mat{T}$, they transform every input signal into the same output signal (\cref{lem:equivsystequivact}).

    This \SY{equivalence relation} is a \SY{congruence} on \LTI (\cref{def:congruence-category}): if $\mat{T}_1$ is an equivalence transformation from $\mora$ to $\mora'$ and $\mat{T}_2$ is one from $\morb$ to $\morb'$, then the block-diagonal matrix
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:LTI-block-diagonal-transformation}
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \mat{T}_1 & \mat{0}   \\
            \mat{0}   & \mat{T}_2
        \end{bmatrix}
    \end{equation}
    is an equivalence transformation from $\mora \mthen \morb$ to $\mora' \mthen \morb'$.
    We can therefore form the \SY{quotient category} $\LTI/_{\equival}$ (\cref{def:quotient-category}).
    Its objects are still the coordinate spaces $\reals^n$, and its morphisms are equivalence classes $\equivmora$ of LTI systems.

    The stacking operations pass to $\LTI/_{\equival}$: on objects nothing changes, and on morphisms we define $\equivmora \mtimescatmor \equivmorb$ to be the equivalence class of $\mora \mtimescatmor \morb$.
    This does not depend on the choice of representatives, since the matrix \cref{eq:LTI-block-diagonal-transformation} is also an equivalence transformation from $\mora \mtimescatmor \morb$ to $\mora' \mtimescatmor \morb'$.

    With these operations and the monoidal unit $\reals^0$, the category $\LTI/_{\equival}$ \emph{is} a \SY{strict monoidal category}.
    Associativity and the neutrality of the unit hold already for representatives, as we saw in \cref{exa:LTI-stacking-not-functorial}.
    And stacking is now functorial: the two systems in \cref{eq:LTI-interchange-two-sides} are equivalent, with the equivalence transformation given by the permutation matrix
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \mat{1} & \mat{0} & \mat{0} & \mat{0} \\
            \mat{0} & \mat{0} & \mat{1} & \mat{0} \\
            \mat{0} & \mat{1} & \mat{0} & \mat{0} \\
            \mat{0} & \mat{0} & \mat{0} & \mat{1}
        \end{bmatrix},
    \end{equation}
    in which the blocks $\mat{1}$ are identity matrices of the appropriate sizes; it exchanges the two middle blocks of the state, as in \cref{eq:LTI-interchange-states}.
    The second condition in \cref{def:functorial-stacking-cat}, on identity morphisms, holds already for representatives.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
    \label{rem:LTI-other-repairs}
    Passing to equivalence classes is not the only way to deal with the fact that the stacking of LTI systems is not functorial.
    Instead of declaring the two systems in \cref{eq:LTI-interchange-two-sides} to be equal, one can keep them distinct and remember the equivalence transformation between them as an additional piece of structure.
    This leads to mathematical structures which are more general, and more complicated, than monoidal categories, in which the conditions for parallel composition only hold ``up to'' specified transformations (see \cref{exa:bicat-lti} for a first step in this direction).
    We do not pursue this here.
\end{remark}






\subsection{When the category is a preorder}
\label{sec:strict-monoidal-preorders}

Recall that every preorder $\posAdefinition$ can be viewed as a category $\poscat{\posA}$ (\cref{def:poscat}): its objects are the elements of $\posAset$, and there is exactly one morphism from $\posela$ to $\poselb$ if $\posela \posAleq \poselb$, and none otherwise.
What is a strict monoidal structure on such a category?
Let us unpack \cref{def:strict-monoidal-cat}.

The monoidal product is a functor $\poscat{\posA} \Ctimes \poscat{\posA} \fto \poscat{\posA}$.
On objects, it is a binary operation on the set $\posAset$, which we write as $\monpostimes$.
On morphisms, note that there is a morphism from $\tup{\posela, \poselb}$ to $\tup{\posela', \poselb'}$ in the product category precisely when $\posela \posAleq \posela'$ and $\poselb \posAleq \poselb'$.
The functor has to map it to a morphism from $\posela \monpostimes \poselb$ to $\posela' \monpostimes \poselb'$, and such a morphism exists precisely when $\posela \monpostimes \poselb \posAleq \posela' \monpostimes \poselb'$.
So the operation $\monpostimes$ must be monotone in both arguments.
Conversely, if it is, then there is exactly one way to define the functor on morphisms, and the two functor conditions hold automatically, because in $\poscat{\posA}$ any two morphisms with the same source and the same target are equal.

The monoidal unit is an element $\idmoncat \setin \posAset$.
The conditions on the level of objects say that $\monpostimes$ is associative and that $\idmoncat$ is neutral for it; in other words, that $\tup{\posAset, \monpostimes, \idmoncat}$ is a \SY{monoid}.
The conditions on the level of morphisms hold automatically, again because parallel morphisms in $\poscat{\posA}$ are equal.

We arrive at the following structure.

\begin{ctdefinition}[Monoidal preorder]
    \label{def:monoidal-preorder}
    A \maindef{monoidal preorder} is:

    \constit
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item A preorder $\posAdefinition$;
        \item A binary operation $\monpostimes \colon \posAset \cartprod \posAset \sto \posAset$, called the \emph{monoidal product};
        \item An element $\idmoncat \setin \posAset$, called the \emph{monoidal unit}.
    \end{enumerate}

    \condit
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Monotonicity: for all $\posela, \posela', \poselb, \poselb' \setin \posAset$,
              \begin{equation}
                  \label{eq:monoidal-preorder-monotonicity}
                  \text{if} \quad \posela \posAleq \posela' \quad \text{and} \quad \poselb \posAleq \poselb', \quad \text{then} \quad \posela \monpostimes \poselb \posAleq \posela' \monpostimes \poselb';
              \end{equation}
        \item Associativity: for all $\posela, \poselb, \poselc \setin \posAset$,
              \begin{equation}
                  (\posela \monpostimes \poselb) \monpostimes \poselc = \posela \monpostimes (\poselb \monpostimes \poselc);
              \end{equation}
        \item Neutrality: for all $\posela \setin \posAset$,
              \begin{equation}
                  \posela \monpostimes \idmoncat = \posela
                  \qqand
                  \idmoncat \monpostimes \posela = \posela.
              \end{equation}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{ctdefinition}

In short, a monoidal preorder is a set which is both a preorder and a \SY{monoid}, in such a way that the two structures are compatible.
By the discussion above, a strict monoidal structure on the category $\poscat{\posA}$ is ``the same thing'' as a monoidal product and a monoidal unit which make the preorder $\posA$ into a monoidal preorder.
When the preorder is a poset, one speaks of a \emph{monoidal poset}\iflabelexists{def:monoidal-poset}{; these will play an important role later on (\cref{def:monoidal-poset})}.

\begin{example}
    \label{exa:monoidal-preorders}
    The following are monoidal preorders, and hence strict monoidal categories:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item the natural numbers with their usual order, addition as monoidal product, and $0$ as monoidal unit;
        \item the natural numbers with their usual order, the maximum of two numbers as monoidal product, and $0$ as monoidal unit;
        \item the Booleans $\Bool$, ordered by $\false \posleq \true$, with $\booland$ as monoidal product and $\true$ as monoidal unit;
        \item the non-negative real numbers $\nonNegReals$ with the \emph{reversed} order $\geq$, addition as monoidal product, and $0$ as monoidal unit (think of costs: there is a morphism from a cost to any cost that is at most as large);
        \item the subsets of a given set, ordered by inclusion, with union as monoidal product and the empty set as monoidal unit.
    \end{itemize}
    In each case, the monotonicity condition \cref{eq:monoidal-preorder-monotonicity} is a familiar fact; for instance, for the first one it says that $\posela \leq \posela'$ and $\poselb \leq \poselb'$ imply $\posela + \poselb \leq \posela' + \poselb'$.
\end{example}

\subsection{When the category is a monoid}
\label{sec:strict-monoidal-monoids}

Recall that every monoid $\monoidA = \tup{\monoidAset, \mtimes, \idmon}$ can be viewed as a category $\monoidcat{\monoidA}$ (\cref{def:monoidcat}): it has a single object $\monoidcatobj$, its morphisms are the elements of $\monoidAset$, composition is the operation $\mtimes$, and the identity morphism is the neutral element $\idmon$.
What is a strict monoidal structure on such a category?
We again unpack \cref{def:strict-monoidal-cat}.

On the level of objects, there is nothing to choose and nothing to check: the monoidal unit must be the only object $\monoidcatobj$, and necessarily $\monoidcatobj \mtimescat \monoidcatobj = \monoidcatobj$.

On the level of morphisms, the monoidal product is a second binary operation on the set $\monoidAset$,
\begin{equation}
    \mtimescat \colon \monoidAset \cartprod \monoidAset \sto \monoidAset.
\end{equation}
That $\mtimescat$ is a functor means that for all $\monela, \monela', \monelb, \monelb' \setin \monoidAset$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:monoid-interchange}
    (\monela \mtimes \monela') \mtimescat (\monelb \mtimes \monelb') = (\monela \mtimescat \monelb) \mtimes (\monela' \mtimescat \monelb')
    \qqand
    \idmon \mtimescat \idmon = \idmon.
\end{equation}
The remaining conditions say that $\mtimescat$ is associative, and that the identity morphism of the monoidal unit, which is $\idmon$, is neutral for it:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:monoid-second-neutrality}
    \monela \mtimescat \idmon = \monela
    \qqand
    \idmon \mtimescat \monela = \monela.
\end{equation}
So a strict monoidal structure on $\monoidcat{\monoidA}$ is a second monoid structure on the same set, with the same neutral element, which is compatible with the first one in the sense of \cref{eq:monoid-interchange}.

This looks like a rich new kind of structure, with two different ways of multiplying.
Surprisingly, it is not: the two operations are forced to coincide, and to be commutative.

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:eckmann-hilton}
    Let $\monoidA = \tup{\monoidAset, \mtimes, \idmon}$ be a monoid, and let $\mtimescat$ be a binary operation on $\monoidAset$ which satisfies \cref{eq:monoid-interchange} and \cref{eq:monoid-second-neutrality}.
    Then, for all $\monela, \monelb \setin \monoidAset$,
    \begin{equation}
        \monela \mtimescat \monelb = \monela \mtimes \monelb = \monelb \mtimes \monela.
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Using, in turn, the neutrality of $\idmon$ for $\mtimes$, the first equation in \cref{eq:monoid-interchange}, and the neutrality of $\idmon$ for $\mtimescat$, we find
    \begin{equation}
        \monela \mtimescat \monelb
        = (\monela \mtimes \idmon) \mtimescat (\idmon \mtimes \monelb)
        = (\monela \mtimescat \idmon) \mtimes (\idmon \mtimescat \monelb)
        = \monela \mtimes \monelb,
    \end{equation}
    and, in the same way,
    \begin{equation}
        \monela \mtimescat \monelb
        = (\idmon \mtimes \monela) \mtimescat (\monelb \mtimes \idmon)
        = (\idmon \mtimescat \monelb) \mtimes (\monela \mtimescat \idmon)
        = \monelb \mtimes \monela.
    \end{equation}
\end{proof}

The resulting structure is therefore one that we already know.

\begin{ctdefinition}[Commutative monoid]
    \label{def:commutative-monoid}
    A \emph{commutative monoid} is:

    \constit
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item A monoid $\monoidA = \tup{\monoidAset, \mtimes, \idmon}$.
    \end{enumerate}

    \condit
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Commutativity: for all $\monela, \monelb \setin \monoidAset$,
              \begin{equation}
                  \monela \mtimes \monelb = \monelb \mtimes \monela.
              \end{equation}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{ctdefinition}

By \cref{lem:eckmann-hilton}, the category $\monoidcat{\monoidA}$ admits a strict monoidal structure only if the monoid $\monoidA$ is commutative, and in this case there is exactly one: the monoidal product of morphisms is the operation $\mtimes$ itself.
(Conversely, if $\monoidA$ is commutative, then $\mtimescat \definedas \mtimes$ does satisfy \cref{eq:monoid-interchange}, since then $(\monela \mtimes \monela') \mtimes (\monelb \mtimes \monelb') = (\monela \mtimes \monelb) \mtimes (\monela' \mtimes \monelb')$.)
In this sense, a strict monoidal category with a single object is ``the same thing'' as a commutative monoid.

For example, the natural numbers with addition, and the real numbers with multiplication, are strict monoidal categories with a single object, while the monoid of $\matdim{2}{2}$ real matrices with matrix multiplication, which is not commutative, cannot be made into one.




\subsection{Theory remarks}


\begin{example}
    Consider the associative stacking category from \cref{ex:assoc-stacking-semicat-integers-max}, where objects are integers and stacking of objects is taking their maximum.
    There is no possible monoidal unit here: it would have to be a neutral element for the operation ``max'', but such does not exist for $\wnumbers$.
    However, we could modify this example, and replace $\wnumbers$ with a bounded set of numbers, such as $\natnumbers$.
    Then the smallest number in $\natnumbers$, namely $0$, serves a neutral element for ``max'' and provides a monoidal unit $\idmoncat$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    Consider the associative stacking category from \cref{ex:assoc-stacking-semicat-lists-concat}.
    A monoidal unit would need to be a neutral element for list concatenation.
    This would be the empty list.
    In \cref{ex:assoc-stacking-semicat-lists-concat} we specified that objects are only non-empty lists, hence we do not have a strict monoidal semicategory.
    However, this example can the be easily adjusted to include also the empty list, in which case we \emph{do} obtain a strict monoidal semicategory.
\end{example}
