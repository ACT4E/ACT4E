\section{Associativity}
\label{sec:associativity}

In the previous section we discussed how the notion of interface is central to series compositions of many different kinds. All of the examples we saw also share a further common feature, which is known as \emph{associativity} (or \emph{the associative property}). This is a generalization of the notion of the associativity that we are already familiar with from numbers systems, semigroups, monoids, etc.

Let us explain associativity of series composition using the electrical chords example. Instead of focusing only on the series composition of two electrical chords, suppose we wish to compose three compatible chords together. 

GRAPHIC


If we connect two composable chords at time, there are two steps needed and two possible variants of how we do it, depending on the order of our actions. On the one hand, we could connect the first two chords together, and then connect their composite to the last chord. 

GRAPHIC

Or, on the other hand, we could first connect the last to chords together, and then connect the first chord to their composite.

GRAPHIC


Either procedure results in the same final outcome, so we might say that the order of our actions doesn't, in the end, matter. That is the essence of associativity: there might be different possible orders in which we implement a nested sequence of pair-wise series compositions, but the overall result will be the same, no matter the route taken. This is analogous to the associativity that we are used to from, say, the addition of numbers; the order in which we add up numbers pairwise doesn't matter for the end result:

\begin{equation}
(\ela + \elb) + \elc = \ela + (\elb + \elc) 
\end{equation}

If we are summing up just three numbers, then their are only two different possible routes to summing them up using pair-wise sums. If we have many more numbers, then their will be more different possible routes, but the still associativity guarantees that the final result from all of them will be the same: 

\begin{align*}
& \ (((\ela + \elb) + \elc) + \eld) + \ele \\
= & \ ((\ela + \elb) + \elc) + (\eld + \ele) \\
= & \  (\ela + \elb) + (\elc + (\eld + \ele)) \\
= & \ \ela + (\elb + (\elc + (\eld + \ele)))
\end{align*}

The same is true for the associativity of series composition: for any finite number of electrical chords that are composable in series, the order in which we compose them pairwise into one long chord doesn't matter for the end result.
