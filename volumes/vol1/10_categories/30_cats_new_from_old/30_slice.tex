\section{Slice and co-slice constructions}
\label{sec:coslice}

\todotext{@J: review and edit}

The final constructions of this chapter again promote morphisms to objects, but this time we do not take \emph{all} morphisms of a category~\CatC: we fix one object~$\slicetarget$ of special interest, and consider only the morphisms \emph{into} it or \emph{out} of it.

\subsection{Slice construction}

The \emph{slice category} of a category $\CatC$ at an object $\slicetarget$ is a general way of modeling ``objects of \CatC equipped with additional information valued in~$\slicetarget$''.
The fixed object~$\slicetarget$ plays the role of a context, a type system, an index, or a base; the objects of the slice are the objects of~\CatC together with their assignment into that context; and the morphisms of the slice are the morphisms of~\CatC that \emph{respect} the assignments.
The examples after the definition make this concrete.

\begin{ctdefinition}[Slice category]
    \label{def:slice-category}

    Let \CatC be a category and fix an object $\slicetarget \setin \Ob_\CatC$.
    The \maindef{slice category} $\sliceCat{C}{\slicetarget}$ \emph{of} $\CatC$ \emph{over} $\slicetarget$ is the category given by:

    \constit

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Objects: morphisms in \CatC of the type $\Obja \mto \slicetarget$, where $\Obja$ ranges over all objects of $\CatC$.
        \item Morphisms: given objects $\Obja \overset{\mora}{\mto} \slicetarget$ and $\Objb \overset{\morb}{\mto} \slicetarget$, a morphism
              \begin{equation}
                  \slicemora_{\sliceCat{C}{\slicetarget}} \colon (\Obja \overset{\mora}{\mto} \slicetarget)
                  \mtoin{\sliceCat{C}{\slicetarget}} (\Objb \overset{\morb}{\mto} \slicetarget)
              \end{equation}
              is specified by a morphism $\slicemora \colon \Obja \mtoin{\CatC} \Objb$ such that the diagram
              \begin{equation}\label{eq:slice-morphism}
                  \includesag{slice-morphism}
              \end{equation}
              commutes.

        \item Composition: defined via the composition in $\CatC$.
              Concretely, given composable morphisms $\slicemora_{\sliceCat{C}{\slicetarget}}$ and $\slicemorb_{\sliceCat{C}{\slicetarget}}$ of $\sliceCat{C}{\slicetarget}$, we define
              \begin{equation}
                  \slicemora_{\sliceCat{C}{\slicetarget}} \mthenof{\sliceCat{C}{\slicetarget}} \slicemorb_{\sliceCat{C}{\slicetarget}} \definedas (\slicemora \mthenof{\CatC} \slicemorb)_{\sliceCat{C}{\slicetarget}}.
              \end{equation}
        \item Identity morphisms: defined by the identities in $\CatC$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{ctdefinition}

\begin{example}[Slices of a poset]
    \label{exa:slice-poset}
    Let~$\posA$ be a \SY{poset}, viewed as a category (\cref{sec:posets-as-cats}), and fix an element~$\slicetarget$.
    Since there is at most one morphism between any two objects, an object of the slice~$\poscat{\posA} / \slicetarget$ is simply an element~$\posela$ with~$\posela \posAleq \slicetarget$, and morphisms are the order relations among such elements.
    The slice is thus the ``down-set'' of~$\slicetarget$: the part of the poset that lies below the chosen element.
    If~$\posA$ orders, say, resource budgets, then slicing over~$\slicetarget$ restricts attention to everything below (or at) the budget~$\slicetarget$. 
    \end{example}

\begin{example}[Typed sets]
    \label{exa:slice-set-typed}
    Fix a set~$\slicetarget$ and consider the slice category~$\sliceCat{Set}{\slicetarget}$.
    An object is a function~$\mora \colon \Obja \mto \slicetarget$, which we can read as a set of items~$\Obja$ in which every item carries a ``type'' or ``tag'' drawn from~$\slicetarget$.
    A morphism from~$\mora \colon \Obja \mto \slicetarget$ to~$\morb \colon \Objb \mto \slicetarget$ is a function~$\slicemora \colon \Obja \mto \Objb$ with~$\slicemora \mthen \morb = \mora$: it may rename or merge items, but it must preserve each item's type.

    For instance, let~$\slicetarget$ be a set of file formats.
    An object of~$\sliceCat{Set}{\slicetarget}$ is a collection of files, each with its format; a morphism might moldel a reorganization of the files (e.g. renaming, deduplication, merging some files of the same format) that leaves every file's format unchanged.
    The slice construction packages ``sets fibered over~$\slicetarget$'' as a category in its own right.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Typed graphs]
    \label{exa:slice-typed-graphs}
    Slices of the category \Grph of graphs (\cref{def:Grph}) are a standard tool for describing networks whose components come in different kinds, and in which only certain kinds of connections are allowed.

    Consider a water distribution plant, built from tanks, pumps, and pipes.
    We describe the admissible ways of connecting these components by a \emph{type graph}~$\mathcal{T}$: its vertices are the three kinds of components, and its edges are the kinds of connections that we allow, in the direction of the flow:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:water-type-graph}
        \begin{tikzcd}[column sep=5em, every arrow/.append style={arrowmorphismstyle}]
            \text{Tank} \arrow[r, bend left=20, "\text{drain}"] \pgfmatrixnextcell \text{Pipe} \arrow[l, bend left=20, "\text{fill}"] \arrow[r, bend left=20, "\text{intake}"] \arrow[loop above, "\text{joint}"] \pgfmatrixnextcell \text{Pump} \arrow[l, bend left=20, "\text{discharge}"]
        \end{tikzcd}
    \end{equation}
    For instance, the edge ``intake'' from Pipe to Pump says that a pump may draw water from a pipe, and the loop ``joint'' says that two pipes may be joined.
    Note that $\mathcal{T}$ has no edge from Pump to Pump, nor from Pump to Tank: in our plants, a pump always discharges into a pipe.

    An object of the slice category $\sliceCat{\Grphname}{\mathcal{T}}$ is a graph $\graphA$ together with a \SY{graph homomorphism} $\tau \colon \graphA \mto \mathcal{T}$, called a \emph{typing}.
    We read $\graphA$ as the graph of a concrete plant: its vertices are the individual tanks, pumps, and pipes, and its edges are the individual connections between them.
    The typing assigns to each component its kind and to each connection its kind of connection, and the conditions for a \SY{graph homomorphism} say that this is done consistently: a connection of kind ``intake'' must go from a pipe to a pump, and so on.
    For instance, the plant in which a tank $t_1$ drains into a pipe $p_1$, from which a pump $u$ draws water and discharges it into a pipe $p_2$, which fills a tank $t_2$,
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{tikzcd}[column sep=2.5em, every arrow/.append style={arrowmorphismstyle}]
            t_1 \arrow[r] \pgfmatrixnextcell p_1 \arrow[r] \pgfmatrixnextcell u \arrow[r] \pgfmatrixnextcell p_2 \arrow[r] \pgfmatrixnextcell t_2
        \end{tikzcd}
    \end{equation}
    is described by this graph together with the typing that sends $t_1, t_2$ to Tank, $p_1, p_2$ to Pipe, $u$ to Pump, and the four edges, in order, to drain, intake, discharge, and fill.

    Note that the typing is additional data, and not merely a property of the graph: the names $t_1, p_1, \dots$ are only labels, and the graph alone does not know which of its vertices are tanks.
    Indeed, the same graph has other typings as well; for instance, sending $t_1$ and $t_2$ to Pipe, the first edge to joint, and the last edge to joint describes a plant in which pipes, not tanks, are at both ends.

    The type graph does the work of a design rule.
    Suppose that we want to describe a plant in which a pump discharges directly into another pump, that is, a typed graph with an edge from a vertex of type Pump to a vertex of type Pump.
    A typing would have to send this edge to an edge of $\mathcal{T}$ from Pump to Pump, and there is none.
    So no object of $\sliceCat{\Grphname}{\mathcal{T}}$ describes such a plant: the rule ``pumps discharge only into pipes'' is enforced by the choice of $\mathcal{T}$, not by an extra condition.

    A morphism in $\sliceCat{\Grphname}{\mathcal{T}}$ from $\tau \colon \graphA \mto \mathcal{T}$ to $\tau' \colon \graphA' \mto \mathcal{T}$ is a \SY{graph homomorphism} $\slicemora \colon \graphA \mto \graphA'$ with $\slicemora \mthen \tau' = \tau$, that is, one that preserves the kinds of all components and connections.
    For instance, the inclusion of a part of a plant into the whole plant is such a morphism.
    So is the map from a plant in which two pumps work in parallel between the same two pipes onto the plant with a single pump in their place, which sends both pumps to the one pump: it describes the plant at a coarser level of detail, without the redundancy.
    On the other hand, a \SY{graph homomorphism} that sends a pump to a tank is never a morphism of $\sliceCat{\Grphname}{\mathcal{T}}$, since it does not preserve types.

    Slice categories of this kind are the foundation of \emph{typed graph transformation}, a well-developed theory and set of software tools for specifying and analyzing systems whose states are graphs (see Ehrig, Ehrig, Prange, and Taentzer, \emph{Fundamentals of Algebraic Graph Transformation}, Springer, 2006).
    There, the states of a system are objects of $\sliceCat{\Grphname}{\mathcal{T}}$, and the rules that rewrite one state into another are expressed through morphisms of $\sliceCat{\Grphname}{\mathcal{T}}$, so that every rewriting step automatically respects the type graph.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[All routes into an assembly]
    \label{exa:slice-free-routes}
    Slicing a free category (\cref{sec:catsfromgraphs}) produces a particularly transparent structure.
    Let $\graphA$ be a graph, fix a vertex $\vertexa$ of $\graphA$, and consider the slice category $\pathcat{\graphA} / \vertexa$.
    An object is a path $\mora$ in $\graphA$ that ends at $\vertexa$, starting at any vertex; this includes the trivial path at $\vertexa$.
    Given two such paths $\mora$ and $\morb$, a morphism from $\mora$ to $\morb$ is a path $\slicemora$ with $\slicemora \mthen \morb = \mora$.
    Since composition in $\pathcat{\graphA}$ is concatenation of paths, this equation says that the path $\mora$ is obtained by appending the path $\morb$ to the path $\slicemora$.
    In other words, $\morb$ is a final segment (a \emph{suffix}) of $\mora$, and $\slicemora$ is the remaining initial segment.
    Two consequences follow:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item A morphism from $\mora$ to $\morb$ exists exactly when $\morb$ is a suffix of $\mora$.
        \item If it exists, it is unique: $\slicemora$ must be the initial segment of $\mora$ that is left over when the suffix $\morb$ is removed.
    \end{enumerate}
    So $\pathcat{\graphA} / \vertexa$ has at most one morphism between any two objects: it is (the category of) a \SY{poset}, namely the set of all paths into $\vertexa$, ordered by ``is an extension of''.
    Moreover, this poset is a \emph{tree}: the paths that a given path $\mora$ extends are exactly its suffixes, and these form a chain, from $\mora$ itself down to the trivial path at $\vertexa$, which every path extends.

    As an example, consider the graph with vertices $a, b, c, \vertexa$ and edges
    \begin{equation}
        e_1 \colon a \to c, \qquad
        e_2 \colon b \to c, \qquad
        e_3 \colon c \to \vertexa, \qquad
        e_4 \colon b \to \vertexa.
    \end{equation}
    The paths into $\vertexa$ are the trivial path $\catidat{\vertexa}$, the edges $e_3$ and $e_4$, and the paths $e_1 \mthen e_3$ and $e_2 \mthen e_3$, and the morphisms of the slice between them (besides the identities, and the composites of the morphisms shown) are:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:slice-free-tree}
        \begin{tikzcd}[column sep=1.5em, row sep=2.5em, every arrow/.append style={arrowmorphismstyle}]
            e_1 \mthen e_3 \arrow[dr] \pgfmatrixnextcell \pgfmatrixnextcell e_2 \mthen e_3 \arrow[dl] \pgfmatrixnextcell \pgfmatrixnextcell \\
            \pgfmatrixnextcell e_3 \arrow[dr] \pgfmatrixnextcell \pgfmatrixnextcell \pgfmatrixnextcell e_4 \arrow[dll] \\
            \pgfmatrixnextcell \pgfmatrixnextcell \catidat{\vertexa} \pgfmatrixnextcell \pgfmatrixnextcell
        \end{tikzcd}
    \end{equation}
    For instance, the unique morphism from $e_1 \mthen e_3$ to $e_3$ is the path $e_1$.

    A natural reading of this example comes from supply chains.
    Let the vertices of $\graphA$ be the intermediate products of a manufacturing process, let its edges be the individual processing and transport steps, and let $\vertexa$ be the final assembly.
    An object of the slice is then a complete supply route into the final assembly, and a morphism from $\mora$ to $\morb$ says that the route $\mora$ continues the route $\morb$ further upstream.
    Routes that share their last steps share a common node of the tree, and the tree branches wherever the supply chain does.

    It is instructive to compare this with the slice of a \SY{preorder} (\cref{exa:slice-poset}).
    In the \SY{preorder} on the vertices of $\graphA$ in which $\posela \posleq \poselb$ means ``there is a path from $\posela$ to $\poselb$'', slicing over $\vertexa$ gives the down-set of $\vertexa$: the set $\makeset{a, b, c, \vertexa}$ of all vertices from which $\vertexa$ can be reached.
    This only records \emph{which} suppliers can reach the final assembly.
    The slice of the free category records in addition \emph{how} they reach it: the vertex $b$ appears in the down-set only once, but it is the starting point of two different routes, $e_4$ and $e_2 \mthen e_3$, which are distinct, incomparable objects of the slice.
\end{example}


\subsection{Co-slice construction}

\begin{ctdefinition}[Coslice category]
    \label{def:coslice-category}

    Let \CatC be a category and fix an object~$\coslicesource \setin \Ob_\CatC$.
    The \maindef{coslice category} $\cosliceCat{C}{\coslicesource}$ \emph{of} $\CatC$ \emph{under} $\coslicesource$ is the category given by:

    \constit

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Objects: morphisms in \CatC of the type $\coslicesource \mto \Obja$, where $\Obja$ ranges over all objects of $\CatC$.
        \item Morphisms: given objects $\coslicesource \overset{\mora}{\mto} \Obja$ and $\coslicesource  \overset{\morb}{\mto} \Objb$, a morphism
              \begin{equation}
                  \slicemora_{\cosliceCat{C}{\coslicesource}} \colon (\coslicesource \overset{\mora}{\mto} \Obja)
                  \mtoin{\cosliceCat{C}{\coslicesource}}
                  (\coslicesource \overset{\morb}{\mto} \Objb)
              \end{equation}
              is specified by a morphism $\coslicemora \colon \Obja \mtoin{\CatC} \Objb$ such that the diagram
              \begin{equation}\label{eq:coslice-morphism}
                  \includesag{coslice-morphism}
              \end{equation}
              commutes.

        \item Composition: defined by the composition in $\CatC$.
        \item Identity morphisms: defined by the identities in $\CatC$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{ctdefinition}

\begin{example}[Open circuits with terminals]
    \label{exa:coslice-open-circuits}
    Co-slice categories are a natural way to describe systems that come with a fixed interface.
    Consider electrical circuits, described as graphs: the vertices are the nodes of a circuit, and the edges are its components (resistors, capacitors, \dots), each with a chosen reference direction.
    To connect a circuit to the outside world, some of its nodes are designated as \emph{terminals}.
    We describe the available terminals by a graph $\mathcal{P}$ that has no edges; for instance, $\mathcal{P}$ has the two vertices $p$ and $q$.

    An object of the co-slice category $\cosliceCat{\Grphname}{\mathcal{P}}$ is a graph $\graphA$ together with a \SY{graph homomorphism} $\iota \colon \mathcal{P} \mto \graphA$.
    Since $\mathcal{P}$ has no edges, such a homomorphism is simply a choice of a node $\iota(p)$ and a node $\iota(q)$ of $\graphA$: it tells us where the terminals are attached.
    (The two terminals may be attached to the same node; this describes a circuit whose terminals are shorted.)
    A morphism from $\iota \colon \mathcal{P} \mto \graphA$ to $\iota' \colon \mathcal{P} \mto \graphA'$ is a \SY{graph homomorphism} $\coslicemora \colon \graphA \mto \graphA'$ with $\iota \mthen \coslicemora = \iota'$, that is, one that sends terminals to the corresponding terminals.
    Such a morphism can be read as a way of finding the circuit $\graphA$ inside the circuit $\graphA'$, compatibly with their interfaces.

    For instance, let $\graphA_1$ consist of a single resistor $r$ from $\iota_1(p)$ to $\iota_1(q)$.
    Let $\graphA_2$ consist of two resistors $r'$ and $r''$ in parallel, both from $\iota_2(p)$ to $\iota_2(q)$, and let $\graphA_3$ consist of two resistors in series, from $\iota_3(p)$ to an internal node $m$ and from $m$ to $\iota_3(q)$.
    There are exactly two morphisms from $\graphA_1$ to $\graphA_2$, sending $r$ to $r'$ and to $r''$, respectively: the single resistor appears in two ways as a branch of the parallel circuit.
    There is no morphism from $\graphA_1$ to $\graphA_3$: a morphism would have to send $r$ to an edge from $\iota_3(p)$ to $\iota_3(q)$, and there is none.
    Nor is there a morphism from $\graphA_3$ to $\graphA_1$: both resistors of $\graphA_3$ would have to be sent to $r$, so the image of the internal node $m$ would have to be both the target of $r$ (which is $\iota_1(q)$) and its source (which is $\iota_1(p)$), and these two nodes are different.
    Unlike in the slice of a free category (\cref{exa:slice-free-routes}), hom-sets of co-slices of \Grph can thus have more than one element.
\end{example}


\begin{example}[The computation tree of a system]
    \label{exa:coslice-computation-tree}
    Dually to the slice of a free category over a vertex (\cref{exa:slice-free-routes}), consider a graph $\graphA$, a vertex $\vertexa$, and the co-slice category $\vertexa / \pathcat{\graphA}$.
    An object is a path $\mora$ that starts at $\vertexa$ and ends at any vertex; this includes the trivial path at $\vertexa$.
    A morphism from $\mora$ to $\morb$ is a path $\coslicemora$ with $\mora \mthen \coslicemora = \morb$: the path $\morb$ is obtained by appending $\coslicemora$ to $\mora$.
    So a morphism from $\mora$ to $\morb$ exists exactly when $\mora$ is an initial segment (a \emph{prefix}) of $\morb$, and it is then unique: $\coslicemora$ must be the final segment of $\morb$ that is left over when the prefix $\mora$ is removed.
    As before, $\vertexa / \pathcat{\graphA}$ is therefore (the category of) a \SY{poset}, ordered by ``is extended by'', and this poset is a tree: the prefixes of a path form a chain, from the trivial path at $\vertexa$, which every path extends, up to the path itself.

    This tree has a familiar interpretation.
    Read $\graphA$ as the transition graph of a (finite state) machine: its vertices are the states, its edges are the possible transitions, and $\vertexa$ is the initial state.
    Then the objects of $\vertexa / \pathcat{\graphA}$ are the finite \emph{runs} of the system, and a morphism from one run to another says that the second run continues the first.
    The tree branches wherever the system has a choice, and it is known as the \emph{computation tree} of the system; in reliability engineering, trees of this kind are known as \emph{event trees}.

    For instance, consider the graph with states $s_0, s_1, s_2$ and transitions
    \begin{equation}
        a \colon s_0 \to s_1, \qquad
        b \colon s_0 \to s_2, \qquad
        c \colon s_1 \to s_0,
    \end{equation}
    with initial state $s_0$.
    The first levels of the tree $s_0 / \pathcat{\graphA}$ look as follows (we show the morphisms between a run and its one-step extensions):
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:coslice-computation-tree}
        \begin{tikzcd}[column sep=1.5em, row sep=2em, every arrow/.append style={arrowmorphismstyle}]
            \pgfmatrixnextcell \pgfmatrixnextcell \catidat{s_0} \arrow[dl] \arrow[dr] \pgfmatrixnextcell \\
            \pgfmatrixnextcell a \arrow[d] \pgfmatrixnextcell \pgfmatrixnextcell b \\
            \pgfmatrixnextcell a \mthen c \arrow[dl] \arrow[dr] \pgfmatrixnextcell \pgfmatrixnextcell \\
            a \mthen c \mthen a \pgfmatrixnextcell \vdots \pgfmatrixnextcell a \mthen c \mthen b \pgfmatrixnextcell
        \end{tikzcd}
    \end{equation}
    Because of the cycle $a \mthen c$ from $s_0$ back to $s_0$, the tree is infinite, although the graph is finite.

    As for slices, it is instructive to compare this with the co-slice of the \SY{preorder} of reachability on the vertices of $\graphA$: there, slicing under $s_0$ gives the up-set of $s_0$, that is, the finite set $\makeset{s_0, s_1, s_2}$ of reachable states.
    The up-set records only \emph{which} states the system can reach, whereas the computation tree records every possible \emph{history} of how it gets there.
\end{example}
